## What you should know about neutrophils

- Neutrophils are your immune system's most abundant first responders — they make up roughly half of all your white blood cells and rush to sites of infection, tissue damage, or stress within hours.
- Recent illness, recent injury or surgery, intense exercise, and acute stress can mobilize them, and most elevations resolve within days to a few weeks.
- Persistent low-grade infections (dental, sinus, gut), chronic inflammatory conditions, smoking, and obesity can keep them elevated longer-term.
- In research on multiple cancer cohorts, EDEAR has observed neutrophil signals in different directions depending on the cancer and the sample type — neutrophils elevated in some research, depressed in others, and shifting in proportion to other immune cells in patterns that research associates with specific conditions.

## What this cell does (the longer-form version)

Neutrophils are the front-line of your innate immune response. When tissue is damaged or infected, neutrophils are typically the first cells to arrive — they migrate out of the bloodstream into tissue within hours, engulf and destroy bacteria and other threats, and release signaling molecules that recruit additional immune cells to coordinate the broader response.

Neutrophils are short-lived, with a typical lifespan of less than a day after they're released into the bloodstream. Because of their rapid turnover, your bone marrow continuously produces fresh neutrophils, and the pace of that production responds quickly to immune demand. The expansion or depression of neutrophils shows up clearly in cellular methylation patterns and in cell-fraction estimates, which is what EDEAR reads.

Other names you may see for these cells in research literature: PMN (polymorphonuclear leukocytes), granulocytes (neutrophils are one type of granulocyte; the others are eosinophils and basophils), polys, segs.

## Where these cells live

Neutrophils are produced in the bone marrow and circulate in blood for hours to a day before migrating into tissue or being cleared. Tissue neutrophils are short-lived and continuously replaced. About 50-70% of your peripheral white blood cells are neutrophils at any given time, which is what EDEAR reads in plasma cfDNA.

## How EDEAR reads them

EDEAR's framework reads neutrophils through the Stage 3 immune cell-type composition lens, using the production Salas IDOL 6-cell reference atlas (which includes a dedicated neutrophil category) and through higher-resolution Stage 3 readings using 19-cell and TIM atlases. Because neutrophils are the most abundant immune cell type in blood, the framework reads them with high confidence and the signal is robust to small variations in deconvolution method.

When neutrophil signal is examined alongside the broader cell-type composition, EDEAR can distinguish patterns where neutrophils elevate alone (suggesting acute inflammation or infection) from patterns where neutrophils elevate alongside monocytes (a broader myeloid expansion) from patterns where neutrophils expand while T cells or B cells contract (the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio shift, which research associates with specific conditions including certain cancers). This last pattern in particular — neutrophils up while lymphocytes down — is the so-called NLR shift that has been studied in many disease contexts.

## Healthy reference range and what affects it

At healthy baseline, neutrophils are the largest fraction of your circulating immune compartment. Your specific reference range is calibrated for your age, sex, and smoking history. Smoking measurably elevates neutrophil activity through persistent low-grade tissue damage in the airways. Obesity is associated with mild persistent neutrophil elevation through metabolic inflammation pathways.

What moves neutrophils around:

- **Recent illness.** Acute bacterial infections elevate neutrophils within hours and the signal resolves over days to a couple of weeks as the infection clears. Viral infections produce a more variable response — sometimes elevation, sometimes depression depending on the specific virus.
- **Recent injury, surgery, or tissue trauma.** Wound healing mobilizes neutrophils for days to weeks.
- **Intense exercise.** Vigorous exercise mobilizes neutrophils immediately, with the signal resolving within hours to a day.
- **Acute stress.** Stress responses (including emotional stress) mobilize neutrophils via cortisol.
- **Persistent low-grade infections.** Dental disease, chronic sinusitis, gut infections, urinary tract infections — keep neutrophils mildly elevated.
- **Chronic inflammatory conditions.** Inflammatory bowel disease (especially during flares), rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and other chronic inflammatory conditions can keep neutrophils mildly elevated.
- **Smoking.** Persistent neutrophil elevation is one of the durable signals of tobacco use.
- **Pregnancy.** Neutrophils elevate substantially during pregnancy as part of the body's adaptation to fetal tolerance.
- **Active cancer.** Some cancers, particularly later-stage and aggressive types, show neutrophil expansion in research.

What does NOT typically drive sustained neutrophil signal: a single bad night of sleep, a single stressful day. The signal responds to sustained or substantial immune drivers.

## Lifestyle interactions

If your neutrophil reading is elevated and you can identify a recent context — a recent illness, surgery, intensive training period, active dental issue, persistent infection — that context is usually the explanation. The signal typically resolves within days to weeks once the underlying context is addressed.

If you cannot identify a recent context and the elevation is sustained, trajectory monitoring is the most useful next step. EDEAR tracks how this signal changes over time. A neutrophil elevation that resolves over your next test was your body recovering. A neutrophil elevation that stays stable points toward chronic context — smoking, obesity, persistent infection, chronic inflammation — worth addressing in conversation with your clinician. A neutrophil elevation that intensifies over time is the pattern that warrants a more direct conversation with your clinician.

## Trajectory patterns

For subscribers with multiple tests, the neutrophil trajectory tells a story that no single reading can. The signal responds within days to weeks to acute resolutions and over months to years to sustained lifestyle changes (smoking cessation, weight management, dental health, persistent infection management).

The trajectory is also informative when it doesn't move. A neutrophil signal that stays elevated across multiple tests despite addressed lifestyle drivers is the kind of pattern that makes a conversation with your clinician more useful than any single reading would be.

A particularly important pattern is the **neutrophil-to-lymphocyte shift** — when neutrophils elevate while lymphocytes (T cells and B cells) decline at the same time. This pattern is different from a simple neutrophil elevation; it represents a rebalancing of the entire immune compartment toward neutrophil dominance. EDEAR flags this pattern when it appears because research has associated it with specific conditions. The pattern is meaningful in trajectory because a stable NLR shift is different from one that fluctuates.

## Research observations on neutrophils

In research on bladder cancer tumor microenvironment, EDEAR's framework observed substantial neutrophil expansion in tumor tissue alongside expansion of nearly every other immune cell type tested — the broad immune infiltration pattern characteristic of bladder cancer in research. Neutrophil signal was one of the strongest in that tumor microenvironment context.

In research on glioma blood cohorts, EDEAR's framework observed a notable neutrophil-to-lymphocyte shift: neutrophil cell fraction increased substantially in glioma patient plasma while CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, B cells, and NK cells all showed concurrent depression. Specifically, the research observed neutrophils shifting upward by approximately 16% of total cell fraction (from around 52% in healthy controls to around 68% in glioma patients), with proportional decreases in the lymphocyte populations.

In research on inflammatory bowel disease cohorts, EDEAR's framework observed something different: neutrophils DEPRESSED while T cells expanded, the opposite direction from the cancer-associated NLR shift. Active Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis research showed substantial neutrophil depression in plasma cell fractions, with concurrent T cell expansion.

In research on long-term breast cancer pre-diagnostic cohorts, neutrophil signal showed a similar attenuating pattern to the broader immune-class signal — strongest in long pre-diagnostic windows (10+ years before clinical diagnosis) and weakening as the diagnosis window approached.

These different research findings illustrate why direction matters. Neutrophil elevation in some contexts and neutrophil depression in others both carry information; the framework reads which direction the signal goes and what other cells are moving alongside it. Customers whose reports show neutrophil signal in either direction can read this page to understand what the directional context means for neutrophils specifically.

## Vigilance and recommended actions

Your report's recommended actions section is calibrated to what flagged. Below is what each tier means for neutrophils specifically.

### If your neutrophil reading is NORMAL

Routine monitoring. Continue the lifestyle habits that work for you. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has discussed with you. Retest with EDEAR at your standard subscription interval.

### If your neutrophil reading is ELEVATED

Review whether any of the common context drivers apply to you over the past few weeks — recent illness, recent surgery or injury, intensive exercise period, active dental or sinus issue, persistent infection, sustained period of acute stress. If something fits, the signal will likely resolve as the context resolves. If you have known smoking history, obesity, chronic inflammation, or a chronic condition like IBD, share this reading at your next clinician visit. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you on the schedule they recommended. Retest with EDEAR in 6 months so trajectory becomes informative.

### If your neutrophil reading is SIGNIFICANTLY ELEVATED — particularly with concurrent lymphocyte depression

We recommend you discuss this finding with your clinician. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte shift pattern (neutrophils elevated while T cells, B cells, or NK cells are depressed) is more informative than neutrophil elevation alone. Bring this report. Particularly relevant contexts to mention: family or personal history of cancer; sustained smoking history; chronic inflammatory conditions; any unexplained symptoms (persistent fatigue, weight changes, persistent fevers, headaches that haven't been evaluated). Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you, on the schedule they recommend, and ask whether they want to adjust anything in light of the EDEAR finding. Retest with EDEAR every 6 months going forward.

We do not recommend specific clinical follow-up tests — those are decisions for you and your clinician, based on your full health history and clinical context that your clinician has and we do not.

### If your neutrophil reading is SUPPRESSED

A SUPPRESSED reading on neutrophils is more notable than for some other cell types because neutrophils are normally the most abundant immune cell. Significant neutrophil depression (sometimes called neutropenia in clinical practice) is most commonly explained by declared context at intake — current chemotherapy (which substantially affects rapidly-dividing cells including neutrophils), certain other medications, certain autoimmune conditions, certain bone marrow conditions, or active inflammatory bowel disease (where the cell-fraction shift moves toward T cell dominance). If you've declared one of these contexts, the SUPPRESSED reading reflects what your body is doing in response. **If you cannot identify a declared context that explains it, this is the kind of finding worth discussing with your clinician sooner rather than waiting for your next routine visit.**

## Frequently asked questions about neutrophils

**What is the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) I sometimes see mentioned?**

The NLR is the ratio between neutrophil count and lymphocyte count in blood. A high NLR (more neutrophils relative to lymphocytes than typical) has been studied as a marker of various conditions in clinical research, including some cancers, infections, and inflammatory states. EDEAR doesn't report NLR as a single number, but the framework reads when neutrophils are elevated WHILE lymphocytes are depressed — that joint pattern is what gives the NLR shift its meaning, and it's what EDEAR flags when it appears.

**My neutrophils are elevated but my doctor's blood test was normal. What's going on?**

Neutrophils fluctuate substantially day-to-day in response to immune context. A standard clinical blood test counts neutrophils at one moment in time. EDEAR's reading is a methylation-based measurement that integrates over the cells produced and released over recent days, so the two readings can show different things at the same moment. If both are normal, your neutrophils are reliably normal. If EDEAR shows elevation and the clinical test is normal, the difference may reflect timing or different aspects of neutrophil biology.

**I have IBD and my neutrophils are depressed. Is that bad?**

In active IBD, research has observed that the cell-fraction balance shifts toward T cell dominance with concurrent neutrophil depression. This is part of the IBD signature, not a separate finding. If you have known IBD and your reading shows this pattern, it's consistent with active disease activity. Your gastroenterologist can put the reading in the context of your IBD management.

**My neutrophils are elevated alongside my other immune cells. Does that mean cancer?**

Not necessarily. Broad immune cell expansion has many possible explanations — active infection, recent vaccination, intensive exercise, chronic inflammation, and many others. Cancer is one possibility but is rarely the most likely explanation for any individual case. Trajectory monitoring distinguishes patterns that resolve (suggesting transient drivers) from patterns that persist (suggesting more sustained contexts that warrant clinician input).

**My neutrophils are elevated AND my lymphocytes are depressed. Is that the NLR shift?**

Yes, that joint pattern is what research calls the NLR shift. Research has associated this shift with multiple conditions including some cancers and some infections. The pattern in trajectory — whether it resolves, stays, or intensifies — is more informative than a single reading. Your clinician can put the pattern in your specific clinical context.
