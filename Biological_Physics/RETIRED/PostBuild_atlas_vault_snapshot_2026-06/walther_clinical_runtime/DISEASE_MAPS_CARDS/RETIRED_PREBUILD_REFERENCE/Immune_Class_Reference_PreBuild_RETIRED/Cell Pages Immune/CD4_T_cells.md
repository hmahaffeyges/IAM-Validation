## What you should know about CD4 T cells

- CD4 T cells are the coordinators of your adaptive immune response — they identify specific threats and tell other immune cells what to do.
- Recent illness, recent vaccination, sustained sleep deprivation, and chronic stress all elevate them, and most elevations resolve within weeks to a few months.
- Persistent infections, autoimmune conditions, and active allergies can keep them elevated longer-term.
- In research on multiple conditions including active inflammatory bowel disease, certain pre-diagnostic cancer windows, and chronic immune activation, EDEAR has observed CD4 T cell expansion as part of broader patterns the immune system shows under stress.

## What this cell does (the longer-form version)

CD4 T cells are the conductors of your adaptive immune response. When your body detects a specific threat — a virus, a bacterium, an aberrant cell — CD4 T cells identify it, organize the response, and signal which other immune cells need to act. They do not directly kill threats themselves; they direct the cells that do (CD8 T cells, B cells, macrophages). Without CD4 T cells, the rest of the adaptive immune system would not coordinate effectively.

CD4 T cells include several functional sub-types: helper T cells that direct general immune responses, regulatory T cells that calm overactive responses (covered separately on the regulatory T cells page), and various specialized subsets that handle specific pathogen types. EDEAR reads CD4 T cells at the broader category level on this page, and resolves regulatory T cells specifically as their own category.

Other names you may see for these cells in research literature: CD4+ T helper cells, Th cells, CD4 lymphocytes, T helper cells.

## Where these cells live

CD4 T cells are produced from precursors in the bone marrow, mature in the thymus, and circulate throughout the body in blood and lymphatic tissue. They concentrate at lymph nodes (where they encounter antigens presented by other immune cells) and at sites of active immune response. About 30-50% of your peripheral blood T cells are CD4 T cells at any given time; their fraction shifts with age and immune state, which is what EDEAR reads.

## How EDEAR reads them

EDEAR's framework reads CD4 T cells through both the Stage 3 immune cell-type composition lens (using the production Salas IDOL 6-cell reference atlas, which includes a dedicated CD4 T cell category) and through higher-resolution Stage 3 readings using a 19-cell reference atlas (UniLIFE) that resolves CD4 sub-types including regulatory T cells, naive CD4, and memory CD4 separately.

The two-resolution reading matters because CD4 T cells move differently when examined as a whole category versus as sub-types. A customer whose total CD4 reading looks normal might still have a regulatory T cell sub-population signal that the framework picks up specifically (covered on the regulatory T cells page). And conversely, total CD4 movement can be substantial when the rest of the immune compartment is steady.

## Healthy reference range and what affects it

At healthy baseline, CD4 T cells are a substantial fraction of your circulating T cell compartment. Your specific reference range is calibrated for your age, sex, and smoking history. CD4 reference ranges shift with age — the proportion of naive CD4 cells declines as more become memory cells through cumulative life exposures, while the total CD4 fraction stays relatively stable.

What moves CD4 T cells around:

- **Recent illness.** Acute infections mobilize CD4 cells within days. The signal stays elevated for weeks as your body finishes the response and converts some cells into memory.
- **Recent vaccination.** Vaccinations specifically mobilize CD4 cells to coordinate the immune memory build. Signal typically resolves over weeks.
- **Sustained sleep deprivation.** Chronic poor sleep elevates CD4 activity through low-grade inflammation pathways.
- **Sustained stress.** Chronic cortisol elevation shifts CD4 methylation. Acute stress events resolve quickly; sustained periods of weeks to months show up clearly.
- **Active autoimmune conditions.** Active autoimmune disease commonly elevates CD4 cells (the disease IS misdirected immune activation in many cases).
- **Allergies.** Active allergic season can elevate CD4 cells managing the allergic response.
- **Chronic infections.** Persistent infections (chronic viral, dental, sinus, gut) keep CD4 cells engaged.
- **Pregnancy.** Pregnancy substantially shifts CD4 activity to allow tolerance of the developing fetus. Reference ranges for pregnant subscribers are adjusted accordingly.

What does NOT typically affect them: a single bad night of sleep, a one-time stressful event, occasional alcohol use, normal seasonal variation. The signal is sensitive to sustained context.

## Lifestyle interactions

If your CD4 T cell reading is elevated and you can identify a recent context — an illness, a vaccination, a sustained stressful period, an active autoimmune flare, a persistent infection — that context is usually the explanation. The signal typically resolves within weeks to a few months once the underlying context is addressed.

If you cannot identify a recent context and the elevation is sustained, trajectory monitoring is the most useful next step. EDEAR tracks how this signal changes over time. A CD4 elevation that resolves over your next test was your body recovering from something it handled. A CD4 elevation that stays stable points toward chronic context worth addressing in conversation with your clinician. A CD4 elevation that intensifies over time is the pattern that warrants a more direct conversation with your clinician.

## Trajectory patterns

For subscribers with multiple tests, the CD4 T cell trajectory tells a story that no single reading can. The signal responds within weeks to acute resolutions and over months to sustained changes (treatment for autoimmune conditions, resolution of chronic infections, improvement in sleep and stress patterns). The trajectory is also informative when it doesn't move — a CD4 signal that stays elevated across multiple tests despite addressed lifestyle drivers is the kind of pattern that makes a conversation with your clinician more useful than any single reading would be.

## Research observations on CD4 T cells

In research on active inflammatory bowel disease cohorts, EDEAR's framework observed substantial CD4 T cell expansion in participants with active Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. CD4 expansion was one of the consistent T-cell expansion findings in the IBD research, alongside CD8 T cell and naive CD8 T cell expansion.

In research on long-term breast cancer pre-diagnostic cohorts, CD4 T cell signal participated in the broader immune-class signal that was strongest at long pre-diagnostic windows and attenuated as clinical diagnosis approached. The regulatory T cell sub-population (a specific functional CD4 sub-type) showed the strongest specific signal — covered in detail on the regulatory T cells page.

In research on Alzheimer's disease blood cohorts, CD4 T cells participated in the broader bidirectional cancellation pattern at the class level — some immune cell types elevating while others depressed — that the framework reads through its directional sub-panel rather than through any single cell.

**The integrated multi-cell pattern stories — IBD population-fraction-shift (Pattern B), the long pre-diagnostic immune-class drift across multiple cancers (Pattern D), and the bidirectional cancellation pattern in Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative conditions (Pattern A) — live on the immune class page in Section 4.** Customers whose reports show CD4 T cell elevation should read both this page (for CD4-specific context, lifestyle drivers, and trajectory framing) and the relevant patterns on the immune class page (for the integrated multi-cell, multi-condition stories).

## Vigilance and recommended actions

Your report's recommended actions section is calibrated to what flagged. Below is what each tier means for CD4 T cells specifically.

### If your CD4 T cell reading is NORMAL

Routine monitoring. Continue the lifestyle habits that work for you. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has discussed with you. Retest with EDEAR at your standard subscription interval.

### If your CD4 T cell reading is ELEVATED

Review whether any of the common context drivers apply to you over the past few weeks or months — recent illness, recent vaccination, sustained sleep or stress disruption, active allergy season, persistent infection, autoimmune flare. If something fits, addressing it is usually enough. If you have a known autoimmune or chronic inflammatory condition, share this reading at your next specialist visit. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you on the schedule they recommended. Retest with EDEAR in 6 months instead of waiting a full year, so trajectory becomes informative.

### If your CD4 T cell reading is SIGNIFICANTLY ELEVATED

We recommend you discuss this finding with your clinician. Bring this report. The conversation is more useful with the result in hand than without. If you have known autoimmune disease, IBD, or chronic infection, share the reading with the relevant specialist. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you, on the schedule they recommend, and ask whether they want to adjust anything in light of the EDEAR finding. Retest with EDEAR every 6 months going forward to track how the signal moves.

We do not recommend specific clinical follow-up tests or specific screening schedules — those are decisions for you and your clinician, based on your full health history, family history, and clinical context that your clinician has and we do not.

### If your CD4 T cell reading is SUPPRESSED

A SUPPRESSED reading on CD4 T cells is most commonly explained by declared context at intake — current immunosuppression, active chemotherapy, transplant status, HIV+ status (CD4 depression is one of HIV's defining clinical signs), certain biologic medications, or rare bone marrow conditions. If you've declared one of these contexts, the SUPPRESSED reading reflects what your body is doing in response to your treatment, condition, or medication. **If you cannot identify a declared context that explains it, this is the kind of finding worth discussing with your clinician sooner rather than waiting for your next routine visit** — CD4 depression has specific clinical significance in some contexts.

## Frequently asked questions about CD4 T cells

**What's the difference between CD4 T cells and regulatory T cells?**

Regulatory T cells are a specific sub-type of CD4 T cells. EDEAR reads them separately because they behave differently from the broader CD4 population in some research patterns. The regulatory T cells page covers them in detail. This page covers the broader CD4 category.

**My CD4 reading is elevated and I have an autoimmune condition. Is that expected?**

Active autoimmune disease commonly elevates CD4 cells in research. If you have known autoimmune disease and your reading is elevated, that's consistent with research expectations. Your specialist can put the reading in the context of your specific disease activity and treatment.

**I just got vaccinated. Should I expect my CD4 to be elevated?**

Yes, often for several weeks after vaccination. CD4 cells coordinate the immune memory build that vaccines trigger. The signal resolves over the following weeks as your body completes the response.

**What does my CD4 cell count from a clinical blood test compare to this reading?**

A clinical CD4 cell count measures the absolute number of CD4 cells per microliter of blood at the moment of the draw. EDEAR's reading is a methylation-based measurement that integrates over the cells produced and released over recent days. The two readings can show different things at the same moment because they measure different aspects of CD4 biology. Customers concerned about HIV monitoring should rely on their clinician's standard CD4 count tests for clinical decisions; EDEAR's reading is complementary, not a replacement.

**My CD4 is elevated but my CD8 is normal. What does that mean?**

T cell subsets can move independently. CD4 elevation without CD8 movement suggests something is engaging the helper-coordinator function specifically — autoimmune activity, allergies, or certain infections often produce this asymmetric pattern. Trajectory monitoring is informative.
