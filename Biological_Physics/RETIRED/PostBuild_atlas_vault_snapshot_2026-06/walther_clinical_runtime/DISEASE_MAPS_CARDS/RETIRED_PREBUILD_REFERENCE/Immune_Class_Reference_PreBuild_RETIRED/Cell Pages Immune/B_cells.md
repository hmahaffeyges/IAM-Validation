## What you should know about B cells

- B cells are your immune system's antibody producers — they make the antibodies that bind to specific threats and mark them for destruction or neutralization.
- Recent illness, recent vaccination, allergies, and ongoing immune challenges all elevate them, and most elevations resolve within weeks to months.
- Persistent infections, autoimmune conditions, and active allergies can keep them elevated longer-term.
- In research on multiple cancer cohorts, EDEAR has observed B cell signals in different directions — elevated in some tumor microenvironment research, relatively preserved when other immune cells deplete in advanced cancer, and participating in broader pre-diagnostic immune-class patterns.

## What this cell does (the longer-form version)

B cells are the antibody factories of your adaptive immune response. When your body identifies a specific threat — a bacterium, a virus, a foreign protein — B cells produce antibodies (also called immunoglobulins) that bind specifically to that threat. The antibodies neutralize toxins, mark pathogens for destruction by other immune cells, and provide a long-term immune memory of the threat.

B cells include several functional sub-types: naive B cells that haven't yet encountered their specific target, memory B cells that retain immunity to past threats, and plasma cells that are antibody-producing factories descended from activated B cells. EDEAR reads B cells at the broader category level, with higher-resolution atlases distinguishing naive vs memory B sub-types.

Other names you may see for these cells in research literature: B lymphocytes, antibody-producing cells, plasma B cells (when referring to plasma cells specifically), CD19+ cells.

## Where these cells live

B cells are produced in the bone marrow and circulate through blood, lymphatic tissue, spleen, and lymph nodes. They concentrate at lymphoid tissue sites where they encounter antigens and are activated. About 5-15% of your peripheral blood lymphocytes are B cells at any given time, which is what EDEAR reads.

## Healthy reference range and what affects it

At healthy baseline, B cells are a moderate fraction of your circulating lymphocyte compartment. Your specific reference range is calibrated for your age, sex, and smoking history. B cell reference ranges shift with age — naive B cell production declines, while memory B cells accumulate from cumulative immune experiences.

What moves B cells around:

- **Recent illness.** Acute infections mobilize B cells within days as your body builds antibody response. Signal stays elevated for weeks to months as your body finishes producing antibodies and converts some cells to memory.
- **Recent vaccination.** Vaccinations specifically mobilize B cells to build antibody-based immune memory. Signal typically resolves over weeks to months.
- **Allergies.** Active allergic responses involve specific antibody types (IgE production) and can elevate B cell activity.
- **Persistent infections.** Chronic infections (chronic viral, gut, dental, sinus) keep B cells engaged producing ongoing antibody responses.
- **Active autoimmune conditions.** Some autoimmune diseases elevate B cells (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's, autoimmune thyroid conditions all involve antibody production).
- **Pregnancy.** Pregnancy substantially shifts B cell activity for both maternal protection and fetal tolerance.

What does NOT typically affect them: a single bad night of sleep, occasional alcohol use, normal seasonal variation. The signal is sensitive to sustained antibody-production demand.

## Lifestyle interactions

If your B cell reading is elevated and you can identify a recent context — a recent illness, a vaccination, active allergic season, autoimmune flare, persistent infection — that context is usually the explanation. The signal typically resolves within weeks to months once the underlying context is addressed or runs its course.

If your B cell reading is depressed (SUPPRESSED tier) without obvious explanation, that's a different kind of finding worth conversation with your clinician. B cell depression in research has been associated with certain immunodeficiency conditions, certain medications (particularly some biologics like rituximab that specifically target B cells), and rare bone marrow conditions. Most often a SUPPRESSED B cell reading is explained by declared context at intake (current biologic medication, immunosuppression); when no declared context explains it, clinical evaluation is the right next step.

## Trajectory patterns

For subscribers with multiple tests, the B cell trajectory tells a story that no single reading can. The signal responds within weeks to months to acute resolutions and over longer periods to sustained changes. The trajectory is particularly informative across vaccination cycles — B cells respond to vaccinations and can be tracked across the months following.

## Research observations on B cells

In research on bladder cancer tumor microenvironment, EDEAR's framework observed substantial B cell expansion in tumor tissue alongside expansion of nearly every other immune cell type tested — the broad immune infiltration pattern characteristic of bladder cancer in research. For customers with plasma cfDNA samples (rather than tumor tissue), the peripheral B cell signal is a different reading than the tumor-microenvironment signal.

In research on advanced gastric cancer cohorts, EDEAR's framework observed an interesting pattern: B cells were relatively preserved (only mildly depressed) while T cells (CD4 and CD8) showed more substantial depression. The B-cell-relatively-preserved pattern alongside T-cell depletion is a specific signature research has documented in some advanced cancer contexts and is one way the framework distinguishes advanced cancer immune microenvironments from other immune patterns.

In research on long-term cancer pre-diagnostic cohorts, B cell signal participated in the broader immune-class patterns the framework reads at the class level.

In research on Alzheimer's disease blood cohorts, B cells participated in the broader bidirectional cancellation pattern at the class level — some immune cell types elevating while others depressed — that the framework reads through its directional sub-panel rather than through any single cell.

Your report's recommended actions section is calibrated to what flagged. Below is what each tier means for B cells specifically.

### If your B cell reading is NORMAL

Routine monitoring. Continue the lifestyle habits that work for you. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has discussed with you. Retest with EDEAR at your standard subscription interval.

### If your B cell reading is ELEVATED

Review whether any of the common context drivers apply to you over the past few weeks or months — recent illness, recent vaccination, active allergic season, persistent infection, autoimmune flare. If something fits, addressing it is usually enough. If you have known autoimmune disease (especially lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's) or chronic infection, share this reading at your next specialist visit. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you on the schedule they recommended. Retest with EDEAR in 6 months instead of waiting a full year.

### If your B cell reading is SIGNIFICANTLY ELEVATED

We recommend you discuss this finding with your clinician. Bring this report. If you have known autoimmune disease, chronic infection, or any history of B-cell related conditions, share the reading with the relevant specialist. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you, on the schedule they recommend. Retest with EDEAR every 6 months going forward to track how the signal moves.

We do not recommend specific clinical follow-up tests or specific screening schedules.

### If your B cell reading is SUPPRESSED

A SUPPRESSED reading on B cells is most commonly explained by declared context at intake — current biologic medication targeting B cells (rituximab, ocrelizumab, others), immunosuppression, certain bone marrow conditions, or rare immunodeficiency conditions. If you've declared one of these contexts, the SUPPRESSED reading reflects what your body is doing in response. **If you cannot identify a declared context that explains a SUPPRESSED B cell reading, this is the kind of finding worth discussing with your clinician sooner rather than waiting for your next routine visit.**

## Frequently asked questions about B cells

**What's the relationship between B cells and antibodies?**

B cells produce antibodies. When activated, B cells differentiate into plasma cells (antibody factories) that produce large quantities of antibodies specific to a threat. Memory B cells retain the ability to mount a rapid response if the threat returns. Antibody levels in your blood are downstream of B cell activity.

**My B cells are elevated and I have an autoimmune condition. Is that expected?**

Yes, especially for autoimmune conditions that involve antibody production (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's, autoimmune thyroid). Active disease commonly elevates B cells in research. Your specialist can put the reading in the context of your specific disease activity.

**I just got vaccinated. Should I expect my B cells to be elevated?**

Yes, often for weeks to months after vaccination. B cells produce the antibody response that vaccines train your body to build. The signal resolves over the following weeks to months as your body completes the response.

**I take a B-cell-targeting biologic medication (rituximab, ocrelizumab, etc). What should I expect?**

Your B cells should be substantially depressed during and for some months after these medications because these drugs specifically target B cells. SUPPRESSED B cell readings are expected and reflect the medication's intended effect. Trajectory across treatment cycles tracks how your B cells recover between doses.

**My B cells are normal but my T cells are abnormal. What does that mean?**

B and T cells can move independently. Selective T cell movement without B cell movement suggests the immune challenge is engaging cellular immunity (T cells) more than humoral immunity (antibodies via B cells). This pattern occurs in viral infections, some cancers, and certain autoimmune conditions.
