## What you should know about plasma cells

- Plasma cells are terminally-differentiated B cells whose only job is to produce antibodies — they are the antibody factories of the immune system.
- Plasma cells live primarily in bone marrow (long-lived plasma cells) with smaller populations in mucosal tissues; the small fraction circulating in blood is what plasma cell-free DNA can read.
- Significant plasma cell elevation in research has been associated with multiple myeloma (clonal plasma cell expansion) — the disease where plasma cells specifically expand while general B cells and naive B cells remain normal or are suppressed.

## What this cell does (the longer-form version)

Plasma cells are the antibody producers. They are terminally-differentiated B cells that have committed entirely to antibody secretion — they no longer divide, no longer respond to new antigens, and no longer carry surface antibody receptors. A long-lived plasma cell in your bone marrow can secrete antibodies for years to decades after its parent B cell first encountered the antigen. Vaccinated immunity that lasts a lifetime is plasma cell immunity. The antibodies that protect you against childhood diseases you had decades ago are produced by plasma cells still living in your bone marrow.

## Where these cells live

Long-lived plasma cells live in bone marrow niches; shorter-lived plasma cells populate the mucosal immune system (gut lamina propria, respiratory tract, urogenital tract). A small fraction circulates briefly in peripheral blood during transit. The plasma cell-free DNA signal is dominated by bone-marrow turnover plus the circulating fraction.

## Why this page covers what it does (grouping reasoning)

Plasma cells appear on a separate page from B cells, naive B cells, and memory B cells because plasma cells and general B cells can move in opposite directions in the same customer. In multiple myeloma, plasma cells expand clonally while general B cells stay normal or DOWN. In B-cell lymphomas, general B cells expand while plasma cells stay normal. The two B-lineage malignancies are the canonical example of why these compartments must be reported separately.

## Research observations on plasma cells

Plasma cell elevation in research has been associated with multiple myeloma — the malignancy where a single plasma cell clone expands and replaces normal bone marrow function, frequently paired with monoclonal antibody (M-protein) elevation in serum and characteristic CRAB findings (hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, bone lesions). In MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance) and smoldering myeloma, plasma cell signal elevation is detectable before overt malignancy develops. Plasma cell elevation has also been associated with chronic infections requiring sustained antibody production (chronic Hepatitis C, chronic parasitic infection), with autoimmune diseases driving autoantibody production (lupus, IgG4-related disease, autoimmune hepatitis), and with the post-vaccination period when plasma cell expansion is the expected biology. In B-cell lymphomas (DLBCL, follicular lymphoma), the opposite pattern — general B-cell expansion while plasma cells stay normal — is the typical finding.

## Frequently asked questions about plasma cells

**Why is plasma cells signal in plasma at the level it is?**

plasma cells cells circulate in blood (or are tissue-resident with measurable cell-free DNA shedding) and contribute methylation signal to the plasma cell-free DNA pool that EDEAR reads. The fraction varies by cell type and by health context — what EDEAR reports is your fraction relative to the age-matched healthy reference distribution.

**My plasma cells signal is elevated and my standard blood work is normal. Should I be concerned?**

Not necessarily. Cellular-architectural signals can precede standard clinical findings by months to years — that's the EDEAR detection window value. Trajectory monitoring is the most useful next step. Most early elevations turn out to be context-driven (recent illness, sustained inflammation, recent vaccination) rather than progressing disease.

**My plasma cells signal is suppressed. What does that mean?**

SUPPRESSED readings have several common explanations: declared immunosuppressive therapy, active chemotherapy, recent transplant, certain medications. Without declared context, sustained suppression warrants a clinician conversation that the trajectory itself supports.
