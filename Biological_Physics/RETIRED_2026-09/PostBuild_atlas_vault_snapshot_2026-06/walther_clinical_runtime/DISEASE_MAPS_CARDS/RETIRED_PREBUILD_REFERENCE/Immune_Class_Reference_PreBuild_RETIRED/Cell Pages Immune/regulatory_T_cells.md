## What you should know about regulatory T cells

- Regulatory T cells help your immune system know when to back off — they keep inflammation in check so the rest of your immune system doesn't overreact.
- Things like sleep, stress, and recent illness affect them, and most elevations resolve within weeks to months.
- Allergies in active season and chronic low-grade inflammation can keep them elevated longer, and addressing the underlying cause usually brings them back down.
- In research on long-term breast cancer cohorts, EDEAR has observed regulatory T cell elevation in blood samples drawn more than 10 years before clinical diagnosis. The signal was strongest at the longest pre-diagnostic windows and got smaller as clinical diagnosis approached.

## What this cell does (the longer-form version)

Regulatory T cells are part of your immune system's calming circuitry. When your body is responding to something — an infection, a stress event, an allergen — other immune cells ramp up. Regulatory T cells make sure that ramp-up doesn't get out of control and damage healthy tissue. When they're elevated, your body is doing more of that calming work than usual.

Other names you may see for these cells in research literature: Tregs, regulatory T lymphocytes, suppressor T cells, FOXP3+ T cells (named for the gene that defines their identity).

## Where these cells live

Regulatory T cells are produced in the thymus and circulate throughout the body in blood and lymphatic tissue. A small fraction lives in your peripheral blood at any given time, which is what EDEAR reads. They concentrate at sites of active immune response — tumors, infection sites, inflamed tissue — to do their calming work where it's needed.

## How EDEAR reads them

EDEAR's framework reads regulatory T cells through two of its three measurement lenses. The first is the Stage 2 cell-of-origin estimation, which detects regulatory T cell DNA fragments in your plasma at low fraction by their methylation signature. The second is the Stage 3 immune cell-type composition estimation, which uses a 19-cell reference atlas (UniLIFE) that includes a dedicated regulatory T cell category alongside the broader CD4 T cell category. The 19-cell resolution is what allows EDEAR to separate regulatory T cells specifically from the larger CD4 T cell population — at lower-resolution 6-cell deconvolution, regulatory T cells aggregate up into the CD4 category and the specific signal is invisible.

This higher-resolution reading matters because regulatory T cells move differently from the rest of CD4 T cells in some research patterns. A customer whose total CD4 reading looks normal might still have a regulatory T cell signal that the framework picks up specifically.

## Healthy reference range and what affects it

At healthy baseline, regulatory T cells are a small fraction of your total immune compartment. Your specific reference range is calibrated for your age, sex, and smoking history.

What moves regulatory T cells around:

- **Sleep quality and consistency.** Disrupted sleep over weeks raises regulatory T cell activity as your body manages low-grade inflammation from poor recovery.
- **Sustained stress.** Chronic cortisol elevation shifts regulatory T cell methylation. Acute stress events tend to resolve quickly; sustained periods of stress over weeks or months show up clearly.
- **Allergies.** Active allergic season can elevate regulatory T cells as your body manages the immune response to pollen, dust, or other allergens.
- **Recent infection.** Regulatory T cells stay elevated for weeks to months after an acute infection as your body finishes recovering.
- **Autoimmune conditions.** Active autoimmune disease commonly elevates regulatory T cells as your body manages the misdirected immune response.
- **Pregnancy.** Pregnancy substantially shifts regulatory T cell activity to allow tolerance of the developing fetus. Reference ranges for pregnant subscribers are adjusted accordingly.

What does NOT typically affect them: a single bad night of sleep, a one-time stressful event, occasional alcohol use, normal seasonal variation in activity. The signal is sensitive to sustained context, not to one-off events.

## Lifestyle interactions

If your regulatory T cell reading is elevated and you can identify a recent context — a stressful month, lingering recovery from illness, allergy season, a new sleep disruption — that context is usually the explanation. The signal typically resolves within weeks to months once the underlying context is addressed.

If you cannot identify a recent context and the elevation is sustained, the most useful next step is trajectory monitoring. EDEAR tracks how this signal changes over time. A regulatory T cell elevation that resolves over your next test is a body recovering from something it handled. A regulatory T cell elevation that stays stable points toward chronic context worth addressing in conversation with your clinician. A regulatory T cell elevation that intensifies over time is the pattern that warrants a more direct conversation with your clinician.

## Trajectory patterns

For subscribers with multiple tests, the regulatory T cell trajectory tells a story that no single reading can. The signal responds within weeks to lifestyle changes — sleep improvements, stress reduction, allergy treatment, recovery from infection — so subscribers who address an underlying context often see the signal trend back toward their baseline within one or two retest intervals.

The trajectory is also informative when it doesn't move. A regulatory T cell signal that stays elevated across multiple tests despite lifestyle changes is the kind of pattern that makes a conversation with your clinician more useful than any single reading would be.

## Research observations on regulatory T cells

In two independent research cohorts (Sister Study and EPIC-Italy, blood samples banked years to over a decade before any cancer diagnosis), EDEAR's framework observed regulatory T cell elevation in participants who later developed breast cancer. The signal was largest in samples drawn 10 or more years before diagnosis and got smaller as the diagnosis window approached.

The research framing matters because regulatory T cells are not commonly tracked in standard clinical workups. They are part of the immune system's modulation circuitry — they suppress excessive immune response to keep inflammation in check, and their elevation in long pre-diagnostic windows is interpreted in research as a signature of the body's modulating its own response to early cellular drift, before any tumor has organized.

Standard clinical detection of breast cancer happens at clinical presentation — when a tumor has organized into a structure that mammography can see. By that point, cellular drift has been accumulating for years and has crystallized into something physical. Methylation changes precede that structural formation. The regulatory T cell signal at long pre-diagnostic windows in research is a measurement of cellular drift before any structure exists, which is a different kind of finding than what standard clinical screening is built to detect.

In the same research cohorts, regulatory T cell elevation was often observed alongside elevation in other immune cells (eosinophils in particular) and mild drift in secretory-class breast cells. As the pre-diagnostic window shortened toward clinical diagnosis, the immune signals attenuated while the secretory-class breast cell signal became more prominent. **The integrated multi-cell pattern story across pre-diagnostic breast cancer cohorts and the broader long-pre-dx pattern across multiple cancer types lives on the immune class page in Section 4 (Pattern D — Long pre-diagnostic immune-class drift across solid cancers).** Customers whose reports show regulatory T cell elevation should read both this page (for cell-specific context) and the immune class page Pattern D section (for the integrated multi-cell, multi-cancer story).

EDEAR's research is ongoing. As an independent research group, we fund continued validation and methodology development through EDEAR subscription proceeds. Our Astro-Genetics methodology — applying calibration techniques from cosmology to cellular methylation data across 37 orders of magnitude — is genuinely unprecedented, and we are extending its reach as additional research becomes possible.

## Vigilance and recommended actions

Your report's recommended actions section is calibrated to what flagged. Below is what each tier means for regulatory T cells specifically.

### If your regulatory T cell reading is NORMAL

Routine monitoring. Continue the lifestyle habits that work for you (sleep, stress management, exercise, the basics). Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has discussed with you. Retest with EDEAR at your standard subscription interval.

### If your regulatory T cell reading is ELEVATED

Review whether any of the common context drivers apply to you over the past few weeks or months — disrupted sleep, sustained stress period, active allergy season, recent illness recovery, active autoimmune condition. If something fits, addressing it is usually enough. Consider continuing the screening recommendations your clinician has given you on the schedule they recommended. Retest with EDEAR in 6 months instead of waiting a full year, so trajectory becomes informative — a signal that resolves was your body recovering; a signal that stays or intensifies is more useful information for a clinician conversation.

### If your regulatory T cell reading is SIGNIFICANTLY ELEVATED

We recommend you discuss this finding with your clinician. Bring this report. The conversation is more useful with the result in hand than without. Continue the screening recommendations your clinician has given you, on the schedule they recommend, and ask whether they want to adjust anything in light of the EDEAR finding. Retest with EDEAR every 6 months going forward to track how the signal moves.

We do not recommend specific clinical follow-up tests or specific screening schedules — those are decisions for you and your clinician, based on your full health history, family history, and clinical context that your clinician has and we do not. EDEAR provides the cellular-level reading; the conversation with your clinician puts it in clinical context.

### If your regulatory T cell reading is SUPPRESSED

A SUPPRESSED reading on regulatory T cells is uncommon and is most commonly explained by declared context at intake — current immunosuppression, active chemotherapy, transplant status, certain biologic medications. If you've declared any of these contexts, the SUPPRESSED reading is expected and reflects what your body is doing in response to your treatment or condition. If you cannot identify a declared context that explains it, discuss the reading with your clinician on your next visit.

## Frequently asked questions about regulatory T cells

**Why are these cells specifically called out and not the broader CD4 T cell category?**

Because they move differently. Regulatory T cells are a small fraction of total CD4 T cells, but in some research patterns the regulatory T cell signal is much stronger than the total CD4 reading. EDEAR uses a higher-resolution 19-cell deconvolution that separates regulatory T cells from the broader CD4 category specifically because the signal would be invisible otherwise.

**Can stress alone really show up in this reading?**

Yes. Sustained chronic stress over weeks to months is one of the most reliable lifestyle drivers of regulatory T cell elevation. Acute stress events resolve quickly; chronic patterns show up clearly.

**My regulatory T cells are elevated and I have allergies — should I be worried?**

Active allergy season is one of the most common benign explanations for regulatory T cell elevation. The signal typically resolves as the season ends. If your reading remains elevated outside of allergy season or after the allergic context has been addressed, that's the situation worth discussing with your clinician.

**I am in active treatment for cancer right now — what does this reading mean?**

Active cancer treatment substantially shifts regulatory T cell activity in research, and the direction depends on the specific treatment regimen. Customers in active treatment should expect their EDEAR readings to reflect treatment effects rather than baseline state. Trajectory monitoring across treatment is informative; a single reading during treatment is not interpretable on its own.

**I've been told regulatory T cells suppress anti-tumor immunity. Doesn't elevation mean something bad?**

Research has explored regulatory T cells in tumor microenvironments and found that they can suppress local anti-tumor immune response, which is a different kind of finding than what EDEAR reads in peripheral blood. Peripheral blood regulatory T cell elevation in research has been observed in many states — chronic inflammation, autoimmune conditions, pregnancy, allergies, infection recovery, and (years before clinical diagnosis) in some cancer cohorts. Most peripheral elevations have benign explanations. The trajectory product is the right tool for distinguishing routine context from sustained signal.
