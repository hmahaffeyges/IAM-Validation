How CPG works (the AstroGenetics method)

The central idea. For the past two decades, the most precise measurement tools in science were developed by cosmologists to read a faint glow left over from the origin of the universe. CPG applies those same tools to the methylation pattern in a single tube of blood. This approach — using the instruments of cosmology to map and score the methylome — is called AstroGenetics, and as far as is known it has not been done before.

What is being read

Every cell in the body carries the same DNA, yet a neuron and a white blood cell behave nothing alike. The difference is methylation: chemical tags on the DNA that switch genes on and off, defining what a cell is and how well it is holding that identity. CPG reads roughly 450,000 of these sites from one blood draw. The question it asks of each cell is not what its DNA is, but how far that cell’s identity has drifted from a healthy reference.

Where the method came from: the cosmic microwave background

Shortly after the Big Bang, the universe filled with light. That light persists today as a faint microwave glow arriving from every direction — the cosmic microwave background (CMB). It appears perfectly smooth, yet it contains differences of roughly one part in 100,000, and the structure and history of the universe is encoded in those minute variations. They cannot be seen directly. Over decades, cosmologists built an arsenal of statistical instruments — full-sky maps, matched filters, and rigorous treatment of uncertainty — to extract a real signal from what looks like noise. The Planck space telescope — the mission that mapped the CMB across the entire sky with extraordinary precision — is the defining example. AstroGenetics adapts that arsenal. The methylome behaves like the same kind of object: a healthy cell sits almost exactly where it should, and disease often appears as differences just as faint as the CMB’s. The appropriate response is not to inspect the map and hope, but to apply the tools built for precisely this problem.

The atlas and its uncertainty layer

Before drift can be measured, the location of healthy must be known — for every cell type, at every one of the ~450,000 sites. That reference is the IAMAtlas, and it was not built by averaging. An average gives a single number and no idea how trustworthy it is. MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo) instead returns, for every site in every cell type, a full probability distribution — a posterior — so that not only the healthy value is known but exactly how sure that value is, and how the cells move together (their covariance). Running that across half a million epigenetic markers in more than 115 types of cells required more than 500 hours of continuous computation. The payoff is the layer no one else in methylation has: every score CPG reports carries an honest 95% confidence interval drawn straight from the atlas’s own posteriors. A poorly-constrained cell returns a wide interval and is treated with caution; a well-constrained one returns a tight interval. The uncertainty is not an afterthought — it is built in, the same discipline the Planck telescope brought to mapping the universe’s oldest light.

Why the IAMAtlas is more than an atlas with error bars

The IAMAtlas is the first reference of its kind derived from the laws of physics rather than assembled from population studies. A conventional reference must be recalibrated for each population — hundreds of thousands of participants studied over many years — and even then it recovers only a direction, the similarities shared across a large group. The IAMAtlas requires none of that. It can be applied to a single person, at any time, anywhere humans exist. It does not measure a sick individual against a large group of study participants constrained to particular parameters; it measures a person against their own cellular operating range, set by the cell itself. One person or fifty, young or old, American or Chinese, cancer patient or Olympic athlete — it does not matter, because human biology fixed that operating range millions of years ago, and CPG simply reads the operator’s manual, which specifies a range for each cell. In summary: a single atlas, more than 150 validation tests to date, more than 80 distinct patterns detected across many cancers and other diseases at various stages of progression — all from one reference. A cohort-based approach would need dozens of separate references, each recalibrated for its own study and its own measured cells, in place of one covering all 115 cell types.

Two jobs, and the line that is never crossed

CPG performs two very different tasks, and keeping them separate is the central discipline of the method.

Measuring a person’s state is physics, and it is self-calibrating. Each cell receives an A-score = H(β) / Hmin: that cell’s own methylation entropy (a measure of how scrambled its identity has become) divided by a fixed informational floor derived from first principles — the lowest order a cell of that type can hold and still be that cell. A healthy cell reads about 1.0; drift toward the floor, or a breach upward, appears as movement away from 1.0. Nothing in that calculation asks how the person compares to a group of others. It asks how far the cell sits from its own floor. Because the number is intrinsic to the person, it transfers — across instruments, populations, and laboratories. This is the part of the method that is never compromised.

Learning what a disease looks like is empirical — but it enters as a direction, never as a baseline. Physics cannot say which way Alzheimer’s pushes the immune compartment; that can only be learned by studying people who have it, and such knowledge is unavoidably cohort-derived. The discipline is in how it is admitted: a disease cohort may supply a direction — a sign indicating which way a condition moves a set of cells — and nothing more. A direction indicates where to look; it is never permitted to become the ruler a patient is measured against. That ruler is always the derived one: the IAMAtlas, the informational floor, the expectation that healthy is 1.0. The moment a cohort’s mean and spread become the standard a patient is measured against, the line between physics and cohort methodology has been crossed — and the known consequence is a model that works only in the population it came from and weakens elsewhere. Methods such as GRAIL and the methylation aging clocks measure a person against a cohort’s distribution; CPG measures a person against a derived floor, which is why a CPG A-score is intended to mean the same thing for a patient anywhere.

What CPG looks for

Two things, together. The first is shape: whether a person’s cellular departures point, as a pattern, the way a known condition points. The second, equally important, is amount: CPG measures how much of each cell type is present against how much is expected, and any cell sitting well above its expected level warrants attention — because an elevated tissue cell can indicate that tissue is shedding into the blood, which can be an early sign of a growing tumor. When a shed cell is abundant enough for its architecture to be read, CPG scores it with the A-score as well. The method does not only search for cells that should not be present in blood; it watches for any cell at a level the body does not expect.

CPG flags and refers; it does not diagnose. The material above is the method, not a verdict. The verdict is the patient’s A-scores read against the reference gauge by a clinician.
