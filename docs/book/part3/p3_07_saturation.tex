\chapter{Four saturation events}\label{ch:saturation}

\section{Two entries per event}

We propose that every actualization event makes two ledger entries \conjecture. The first
goes to the \emph{local} encoding surface, which is hot and finite: the transistor junction,
the qubit's substrate and barrier, the chromatin surface of a nucleus, or a forming
black-hole horizon. The second goes to the \emph{cosmic} horizon, which is cold and, on
observable timescales, never saturates. Information migrates from the first to the second at
a rate set by their temperature difference. A local surface can saturate; the cosmic one
cannot.

In standard quantum mechanics the first entry has a name. Decoherence is the environment acquiring records of a system's state: the
interference that would reveal a superposition is lost into correlations with the surroundings, and the states that survive are those
the environment can record without disturbing them~\cite{Zurek2003} \derived. A qubit that decoheres has had its state copied into its
environment \derived. The ledger reading adds the second entry, the migration of those records outward to the cosmic horizon \conjecture; for a
qubit inside a cryostat that path runs through the shields and stages around it, not through the sky. \interp

\paragraph{No threshold in the drive ratio of a black hole.} The black-hole row is a saturation of area, not of the
drive ratio. With $\Edrive=M_{\rm BH}c^2$ at the Hawking temperature $T_H=\hbar c^3/(8\pi G M_{\rm BH}\kB)$ the drive
ratio is $\mathcal M=M_{\rm BH}c^2/\kB T_H=8\pi G M_{\rm BH}^2/\hbar c=2S_{\rm BH}/\kB$, which is Smarr's relation
$M_{\rm BH}c^2=2T_HS_{\rm BH}$ for a non-rotating hole~\cite{Smarr1973} \derived. It grows as $M_{\rm BH}^2$ with no
critical value: for one solar mass $T_H=6.2\times10^{-8}$~K and $\mathcal M=2.1\times10^{77}$ \calc. What marks the horizon is
the area bound $S=A/4\lP^2$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:blackholes}). A hole is colder than the cosmic microwave background, and
gains more energy from it than it radiates, unless $M_{\rm BH}<\hbar c^3/(8\pi G\kB T_{\rm CMB})=4.5\times10^{22}$~kg
\calc.

The common structure is real at the level of form \interp. In each case:
\begin{enumerate}
\item there is a surface on which irreversible events are recorded;
\item the surface has a temperature: $\hbar\kappa/2\pi\kB c$, $T_j$, the qubit's own temperature $T_q$ or $T_{\mathrm{body}}$;
\item there is a floor below which a record cannot be written or kept, and a regime change
      when the system is driven against it.
\end{enumerate}
A stronger proposal is that each coefficient is a ratio of a topological
period to a geometric normalization, as $\tfrac14=2\pi/8\pi$ is for gravity \conjecture. For gravity the ratio is
derived (Chapter~\ref{ch:bekenstein}). For the chip and the qubit the floors themselves follow from $\kB T$ at the temperature the record
sees \derived; what is set from data is where each device sits above its floor, and for the cell the height of the floor, through the measured
holding energy. The four thresholds therefore stand as a unified \emph{picture} whose floors are derived in three places and measured in one.
Turning the picture into a theory requires deriving the remaining coefficients (the cell's holding energy, or the constants of the coherence
optimum) from surface geometry, before comparing them with data \openprob.

The biological application is the subject of Part~\ref{part:4}, and only its structure is needed here. The methylation state of a CpG is a binary record
written on chromatin, copied at every division. A cell's copy error $\varepsilon$, read on single molecules, is set against the thermal floor
$\varepsilon_0=1/(1+e^{E_{\rm hold}/\kB T})$ for a measured holding energy $E_{\rm hold}=3.41\,\kB T$ \measured, at the cell's architecture position $P$:
$A=H(\varepsilon)/(P\,H(\varepsilon_0))$ (IAM-A). On arrays, the same cell is read against the healthy cell of its own kind (Met-A). Both read
1 for a healthy cell, and both are per cell. Part~\ref{part:4} builds the instrument; the point here is only why a
physicist should expect a Landauer-type floor on a chromatin surface in the first place.

The same saturation picture runs through all four places. A chip engineer, a quantum-device physicist or a geneticist can take their own row and work on writing its ratio down from the geometry of its surface; each one written down strengthens the whole ladder.
