\chapter{Landauer at the transistor gate: CMOS and the Dennard wall}\label{ch:cmos}

\section{The floor for logic}

A conventional logic gate erases information at almost every operation. The Landauer floor at
a junction temperature $T_j=348$~K (75~$^\circ$C) is
\begin{equation}
 \kB T_j\ln2=3.33\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J=0.021\ \mathrm{eV}.
\end{equation}
Real CMOS switches at the fJ--aJ scale per gate~\cite{Theis2017}. Using whole-chip power
divided by transistor count and clock, $\Edrive=\mathrm{TDP}/(N_{\mathrm{trans}}f)$, the Apple M1
spends $3.90\times10^{-19}$~J per transistor per cycle, which gives $M=\Edrive/\kB T_j=81.2$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:iams_law}). Most transistors do not switch every cycle, which raises the energy per
actual switch, while TDP also contains leakage and static power, which lowers it; $M=81$ is an
order-of-magnitude figure.
Either way, present logic runs one to two orders of magnitude above $\kB T$, which is far
closer to the Landauer floor than a naive fJ estimate suggests. This is the regime in which
thermal physics, not lithography, sets the rules.

\section{Dennard scaling and its end}

Dennard \emph{et al.}\ showed in 1974 that shrinking all dimensions and the supply voltage by
$1/\kappa$ keeps power density constant, while delay falls as $1/\kappa$ and density rises
as $\kappa^2$~\cite{Dennard1974}. The rule held until the mid-2000s. It failed because the
supply voltage cannot fall below a few tenths of a volt without leakage becoming prohibitive.
The subthreshold swing of a MOSFET is bounded below by $(\kB T/q)\ln10=60$~mV/decade at
300~K~\cite{Sze2006}, so the threshold voltage cannot be scaled without an exponential rise
in off-current. Once $V$ is held fixed, power density grows as $\kappa^2$
(Figure~\ref{fig:dennard}). Clock frequencies stalled near 2005, and the industry turned to
multicore designs and, later, dark silicon~\cite{Esmaeilzadeh2011}. Koomey's law, the doubling
of computations per joule, slowed from every 1.57 years to about every 2.6 years~\cite{Koomey2011,Koomey2016}.

\begin{keybox}[title={The Dennard wall is a Boltzmann wall}]
The 60~mV/decade limit is $\kB T/q$ times $\ln10$. The end of Dennard scaling is therefore
a statement that switching energies approached a fixed multiple of $\kB T$ at the junction.
That is the regime IAM describes: an encoding surface (the gate channel) at a definite
temperature ($T_j$), with a floor set by $\kB T_j$. The identification is standard device
physics, and IAM's contribution is to place it on the same axis as the horizon and the qubit.
\end{keybox}

\section{The the semiconductor report observations}

the semiconductor report Issue~002~\cite{MahaffeySCAPE} tracks the energy efficiency of CPUs and SoCs from
published specifications. Its physical observations, restated in terms of $M$ and the
per-step improvement, are these.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{The 2005 regime change from 2003--2005 data alone.} Pentium~4 to Core~2
       (2003--06) delivered an 81.6\% step, and Core~2 to Core~i7-920 (2008) delivered 28.4\%.
       A constant step of the earlier size would have driven the chip through its own floor in
       about 3.5 nodes. the semiconductor report reads that physical impossibility as the signal of a regime change.
       \calc\ The logic is sound: a step size that cannot continue must stop. It locates the
       Dennard break at the right time from before-the-fact data.
 \item \textbf{A Dennard amplifier.} The ratio of the pre-2005 per-node gain to the present one
       (72\% to 26.6\% for Apple Silicon) is $g=2.71$. \calc
 \item \textbf{Temperature response by architecture class.} Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:npower} in
       junction temperature, the semiconductor report assigns $n=1.853$ to pre-Dennard CMOS, $0.936$ to
       post-Dennard Intel, $0.340$ to AMD and $0.794$ to Apple Silicon. \calibrated\ With these
       exponents Intel and AMD, within 1\% of each other at 75~$^\circ$C, cross at
       81~$^\circ$C, and at 105~$^\circ$C AMD is 3.7\% more efficient.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{checkbox}
The crossover follows from the two exponents alone. With efficiency indices $I_{\mathrm A}=576$
and $I_{\mathrm I}=570$ at $T_0=348.15$~K, $I(T)=I_0(T/T_0)^n$ gives
$(T/T_0)^{0.936-0.340}=576/570$, so $T=354.3$~K$=81.2$~$^\circ$C, and at 378.15~K the indices are
592 and 616. This reproduces the source (82~$^\circ$C; 592$\times$, 615$\times$). The physics of
why energy per operation should rise as a power of $T_j$ with a class-specific exponent is not
derived. Leakage rises exponentially with $T$, and dynamic energy rises weakly. $n$ is the local
slope of the mix, and it is \calibrated.
\end{checkbox}

\section{Architecture overhead}

the semiconductor report separates an architecture-locked component $A_{\mathrm{isa}}$, the overhead of the
instruction-set architecture and system design, which no node shrink removes, from an
engineering-accessible component $A_{\mathrm{node}}$. It reports that Apple Silicon is about
8.7 times more efficient than x86 at the same foundry and node. It attributes the gap to system
integration (unified memory, package co-design, dedicated accelerators) rather than to the ARM
instruction set itself, since Qualcomm's ARM parts sit between the two. \conjecture\ The
decomposition is useful. The attribution is plausible and consistent with independent
analyses showing that ISA per se matters little for energy~\cite{Blem2013}.

\section{Prediction}

\prediction\ The next Dennard-equivalent transition occurs in 2026--2028. In falsifiable form:
AMD's and Intel's published 2~nm-class improvement steps will be materially smaller than their
most recent steps. Intel's Arrow Lake step (70.7\%, the largest in its twenty-year record)
would, if held, reach the x86 floor in 1.7 node steps, so it cannot hold.
