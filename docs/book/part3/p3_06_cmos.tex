\chapter{Landauer at the transistor gate: CMOS and the Dennard wall}\label{ch:cmos}

\section{The floor for logic}

A conventional logic gate erases information at almost every operation. The Landauer floor~\cite{Landauer1961} at
a junction temperature $T_j=348$~K (75~$^\circ$C) is
\begin{equation}
 \kB T_j\ln2=3.33\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J=0.021\ \mathrm{eV}.
\end{equation}
The chip gauge (Chapter~\ref{ch:ascoreqc}) uses whole-chip power divided by transistor count and clock,
$E_{\mathrm{sw}}=\mathrm{TDP}/(N_{\mathrm{trans}}f)$. For the AMD Ryzen~9 9950X (default TDP 170~W, base clock 4.3~GHz~\cite{AMD9950X};
$N_{\mathrm{trans}}=(20.0$--$20.6)\times10^9$ transistors in two compute dies and an I/O die) this gives $E_{\mathrm{sw}}=(1.92$--$1.97)\times10^{-18}$~J
per transistor per cycle, 576--593 times $\kB T_j\ln2$, or $M=E_{\mathrm{sw}}/\kB T_j=399$--$411$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:iams_law}) \calc. The
floor therefore sits at 0.0017 on the chip gauge. Most transistors do not switch every cycle,
which raises the energy per actual switch, while TDP also contains leakage and static power, which lowers it; the reading is an
order-of-magnitude figure for the whole chip. The transistor count is the least certain input: the maker has published no count for this part, and the two figures in
circulation, $20.0\times10^9$ and $20.6\times10^9$, come from die-level reports, so the reading is given as the range they span \openprob. Present logic therefore runs a few hundred $\kB T$ above the thermal noise quantum per
switch on this average. This is the regime in which thermal physics, not lithography, sets the rules.

\section{Dennard scaling and its end}

Dennard \emph{et al.}\ showed in 1974 that shrinking all dimensions and the supply voltage by
$1/\kappa$ keeps power density constant, while delay falls as $1/\kappa$ and density rises
as $\kappa^2$~\cite{Dennard1974}. The rule held until the mid-2000s. It failed because the
supply voltage cannot fall below a few tenths of a volt without leakage becoming prohibitive.
The subthreshold swing of a MOSFET is bounded below by $(\kB T/q)\ln10=60$~mV/decade at
300~K~\cite{Sze2006}, so the threshold voltage cannot be scaled without an exponential rise
in off-current. Once $V$ is held fixed, power density grows as $\kappa^2$
(Figure~\ref{fig:dennard}). Clock frequencies stalled near 2005, and the industry turned to
multicore designs and, later, dark silicon~\cite{Esmaeilzadeh2011}. Koomey's law, the doubling
of computations per joule, slowed from every 1.57 years to about every 2.7 years~\cite{Koomey2011,Koomey2016}.

\begin{keybox}[title={The Dennard wall is a Boltzmann wall}]
The 60~mV/decade limit is $\kB T/q$ times $\ln10$. The end of Dennard scaling is therefore
a statement that switching energies approached a fixed multiple of $\kB T$ at the junction.
That is the regime IAM describes: an encoding surface (the gate channel) at a definite
temperature ($T_j$), with a floor set by $\kB T_j$. The identification is standard device
physics, and IAM's contribution is to place it on the same axis as the horizon and the qubit.
\end{keybox}

\paragraph{Which operations pay the floor.} The Landauer cost is owed by logically irreversible steps, those that
erase information. Logic built from reversible steps can in principle switch for less than $\kB T\ln2$ per
operation~\cite{Bennett1973,Bennett1982}. Conventional CMOS erases at almost every gate, so the floor applies to it.

\paragraph{The reliability floor.} A second and larger floor comes from reliability. A binary switch whose two states
are separated by a barrier $E$ is flipped by thermal noise with probability $p\approx e^{-E/\kB T}$, so an error
probability $p$ per operation needs
\begin{equation}
 E_{\min}=\kB T\ln(1/p)
 \label{eq:reliableswitch}
\end{equation}
\cite{Zhirnov2003}. At $p=\tfrac12$ this is $\kB T\ln2$, the Landauer floor itself. At $p=10^{-15}$ it is
$34.5\,\kB T$, or 49.8 Landauer units, and at $p=10^{-25}$ it is 83.0 units \calc\ (Figure~\ref{fig:switch_floors}a).
The multiple of $\kB T\ln2$ that a working switch needs is set by the error rate the design accepts.

\paragraph{What Dennard scaling gave per node.} Under constant-field scaling~\cite{Dennard1974} a node step divides
every length and the supply voltage by $\kappa$. Capacitance and voltage both fall by $1/\kappa$, so the switching
energy $CV^2$ falls as $\kappa^{-3}$. For $\kappa=\sqrt2$, which halves the area per transistor, that is a 64.6\,\%
reduction per node. At fixed voltage it is 29.3\,\% \derived\ (Figure~\ref{fig:switch_floors}b).

\section{The chip gauge over time}

Restated on the chip gauge, the history of CMOS efficiency carries three physical observations.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{A step series that would cross the floor must end.} The reading cannot fall below the Landauer floor. A constant
       fractional improvement per node, continued, would carry a chip through its own floor in a finite number of nodes; a step size
       that cannot continue must stop. The end of Dennard scaling is where the per-node steps of CMOS efficiency shrank \interp.
 \item \textbf{The floor moves with the junction.} $\kB T_j\ln2$ is linear in the junction temperature: it is 8.6\,\% higher at
       105\,$^\circ$C than at 75\,$^\circ$C \calc, and the reliability floor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reliableswitch} moves in the same proportion
       (Chapter~\ref{ch:chipgen}).
 \item \textbf{The chip's own energy moves differently.} Leakage rises exponentially with $T$, and dynamic energy rises weakly. How a
       design's energy per switch responds to temperature is a property of that design at its operating point, measured on the design and
       not part of the floor \interp.
\end{enumerate}

The chip gauge divides a power figure by the total transistor count and one clock frequency. Three properties of that
construction limit what a difference between architectures can carry. The power figures differ in kind: a base power
for some processors, a maximum turbo power for others, a board power for accelerators, and estimates for systems-on-chip
whose makers publish none. The transistor count includes cache, graphics and accelerator blocks, most of which do not
switch every cycle. The chips also run at different points on their voltage--frequency curves, and dynamic energy
scales as $CV^2$. A gap between architectures on this gauge is therefore confounded with operating point and die
composition. Its attribution to system integration rather than to the instruction set is a \conjecture; independent
measurements find that the instruction set itself matters little for energy~\cite{Blem2013}. The clock alone can move
the reading: one die at one board power, quoted at 1.83 and at 1.98~GHz, reads $1-1.83/1.98=7.6\,\%$ more efficient
at the higher clock with no change in energy per switch \calc.
Separating a node component from an architecture component on the chip gauge needs per-block power and activity data from
the designs \openprob.

\noindent The power definition moves the reading as much as the clock does. A generation pair whose power figure changes from a 253~W maximum
turbo rating to a 125~W base rating reads a 70.7\,\% step; on one definition for both chips the same pair reads 40.7\,\% \calc. The floor itself
moves with the chip's own junction temperature, by 8.6\,\% between 75 and 105\,$^\circ$C \calc. Both are treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:chipgen}.

\section{What the gauge offers a designer}
On one stated definition of power, count and clock, the chip gauge gives one number per design: how many Landauer floors the design spends per
transistor per cycle at its own junction temperature. Read along a design line, it shows where each generation's gain came from and how much
room is left before the floor; Chapter~\ref{ch:chipgen} sets out the definitions, the node-step references and the design families \interp.
