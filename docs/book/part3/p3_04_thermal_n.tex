\chapter{The thermal coupling exponent $n$}\label{ch:thermaln}

\section{The scaling law}

The device gauges of Chapter~\ref{ch:ascoreqc} describe how a platform's error responds to its operating temperature by
\begin{equation}
 A(T)=A_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{n},
 \label{eq:npower}
\end{equation}
with one exponent $n$ per \emph{architecture class}, meaning per dominant irreversible mechanism.
The argument for the power-law form runs as follows. The Landauer cost per event is
linear in $T$; the rate of events has its own temperature dependence set by the mechanism;
their product is locally a power law \conjecture. The form is exact for mechanisms with
power-law densities of states, for example Raman spin--lattice relaxation ($T^7$, $T^9$) or
one-phonon processes ($T^1$ at high $T$)~\cite{Abragam1970}. For activated mechanisms
($e^{-E_a/\kB T}$) it is only a local description, whose logarithmic slope
$n=E_a/\kB T$ varies with $T$.

\section{A thermal-emission benchmark}

Decoherence of C$_{70}$ matter waves by thermal emission~\cite{Hackermuller2004} shows the physics directly. Laser heating
before a Talbot--Lau interferometer raises the internal temperature from about 1000 to
3000~K. The resulting emission of visible photons carries which-path information and destroys
the fringes. From the visibilities in Figure~4 of that experiment, at the mean temperatures given there, between
1360 and 3140~K, the local slope is $n_{\mathrm{C70}}=7.64\pm0.88$ by bootstrap over pairs of points \calc.

\begin{checkbox}[title={Independent check: what the C$_{70}$ data can and cannot supply}]
\begin{itemize}
\item Figure~4 of the experiment plots visibility against \emph{heating laser power}. Its mean temperatures come from
      the model of heating and radiative cooling, and are not measured directly.
\item The emission rate of hot C$_{70}$ is thermally activated: it is negligible below about 2000~K, and the spectrum is cut off
      at long wavelengths by the lack of radiative transitions below $\sim1.5$~eV~\cite{Hackermuller2004}. An exponent of $7.6$ over
      1360--3140~K is therefore a local slope of an activated process, and it would be different over a different window.
      \calc\ in the sense of a slope, not a universal constant.
\item Decoherence by gas collisions is linear in gas \emph{pressure}~\cite{Hornberger2003}: one event per collision, linear in the
      density of the collision partners. At fixed pressure its temperature dependence goes as $n_{\mathrm{gas}}\bar v\propto T^{-1/2}$,
      and at fixed density as $T^{+1/2}$ \derived. It does not supply an exponent of 1 in temperature.
\end{itemize}
The C$_{70}$ experiment shows that thermal emission converts a quantum object into a
classical one, which is exactly the physics IAM is about. It does not fix the exponents of
qubit platforms.
\end{checkbox}

\section{A geometric reading}\label{sec:geom}

One reading of $n$ is as encoding-surface geometry, by analogy with
$\tfrac14=2\pi/8\pi$ in Chapter~\ref{ch:blackholes}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $n=1$ corresponds to single-step coupling at a strict two-dimensional surface.
\item $n>1$ arises from cascades, with $n\approx2$ for two sequential thermal steps as in the NV
      spin--phonon process, and from a second active surface, as with the coupler junction of CZ
      gates.
\item $n<1$ arises when a three-dimensional coupling volume feeds a two-dimensional encoding
      shell: the Rydberg blockade sphere and the motional wavefunction of an ion.
\end{itemize}
In this picture the ordering in Table~\ref{tab:n} is monotonic in coupling depth and surface
dimensionality \conjecture. The ordering is qualitatively sensible and gives a physical
vocabulary for the exponents. The specific decimal values are not derived from it. Fractional parts read as a
``fractal'' excess of the AlO$_x$ interface or a spin--strain factor would be fitted to the calibrated $n$, not predicted.
The test is whether the geometric reading predicts $n$ for a new class before its data exist. The neutral-atom class,
whose exponent was assigned before data, is the next opportunity \openprob.

\section{Why $n$ matters in practice}

Equation~\eqref{eq:npower} says that cooling pays in proportion to $2^n$ per halving. For a
CZ transmon a factor 2.7 improvement per halving of the effective TLS temperature is
substantial. For a laser-gate ion the factor is 1.5, and cooling is not the lever: gate design
and laser control are \calc. This conclusion holds regardless of how $n$ is interpreted.
