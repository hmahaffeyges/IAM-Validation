\chapter{The gate error and its floor: quantum processors}\label{ch:ascoreqc}

\section{Two device gauges}\label{sec:devicegauges}

IAM's Law prices every irreversible record at $\kB T\ln2$ per bit at the surface where it is written (Chapter~\ref{ch:iams_law}). Applied to devices,
the law gives two gauges, used in this and the following chapters of Part~\ref{part:3}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The qubit gauge} reads a processor's two-qubit gate error as information lost per gate, $\varepsilon=-\ln(1-p_{2Q})$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:Agate}), against the \emph{material floor} $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}$, the lowest error the dominant loss mechanism of that
platform allows. The device as built reads $A=1$; its floor sits below it at $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}/\varepsilon$, and more error lies to the right.
\item \textbf{The chip gauge} reads a processor's switching energy per transition, $E_{\mathrm{sw}}$, against the Landauer floor
$\kB T_j\ln2$ at the junction temperature $T_j$. The chip as built reads $A=1$; the Landauer floor sits below it at $\kB T_j\ln2/E_{\mathrm{sw}}$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cmos}).
\end{itemize}
Both gauges read one object against two things that belong to it: its own as-built state and the floor physics sets for it. Chapter~\ref{ch:onegauge}
(Part~\ref{part:5}) sets them beside the cell gauges of Part~\ref{part:4}.

\section{From gate error to information cost}

A two-qubit gate with error probability $p$ preserves the intended state with probability
$1-p$. The information lost per gate, in nats, is
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon=-\ln(1-p)=p+\tfrac12p^2+\dots
 \label{eq:Agate}
\end{equation}
For every platform of current interest $p<10^{-2}$, so $\varepsilon$ equals $p$ to better
than 0.5\% \calc. The gate error in nats, $\varepsilon$, is therefore a re-expression of the published error, and the ranking
it produces is the ranking by two-qubit error. Its value lies in the \emph{decomposition} and in
the \emph{floor} it is compared with, not in the transformation itself.

\section{The published values}

the whole table. First, published two-qubit errors mix isolated and simultaneous benchmarks,
randomized and cross-entropy benchmarking, and median and mean over the device, and different benchmarks of one processor can give
materially different pictures~\cite{Proctor2022}; differences at the 20\% level are not meaningful without harmonized definitions. Second, the
``operating temperature'' of an ion or atom platform is its motional temperature, not a cryostat stage.

\section{Floors}

For each platform the qubit gauge assigns a \emph{material floor} $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}$, the error
below which the dominant physical mechanism of that platform does not permit a gate to go, and
$\varepsilon/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}$ says how far a device sits above its floor. On the gauge the
device as built reads $A=1$ and its floor sits below it at $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}/\varepsilon$: for Helios, $10^{-4}/7.9\times10^{-4}=0.13$;
for Willow, $10^{-3}/3.3\times10^{-3}=0.30$ \calc. The floors are:
Yb$^+$ laser gates $3\times10^{-4}$; Ba$^+$ laser gates $1\times10^{-4}$; electronically driven ion gates
$3\times10^{-5}$; Al-based transmons $\sim1\times10^{-3}$ with an engineered participation ratio and $\sim3\times10^{-3}$ without;
Ta-based transmons $\sim3\times10^{-4}$; neutral-atom Rydberg gates $5\times10^{-3}$; photonic $\sim0.05$.
Each floor is assigned per mechanism from oxide stoichiometry and defect physics, not fitted as a line through hardware data. The
assignment is not yet a derivation, so the floors are \calibrated.

\section{The threshold picture}

Surface-code error correction needs physical error below about $10^{-2}$, and useful
fault-tolerant overhead needs about $10^{-3}$ or better~\cite{Fowler2012,GoogleWillow2025}.
The qubit gauge places each device on one axis with three marks: its material floor, the fault-tolerance target and the error-correction threshold.
Helios reads $A=1$ with its floor at $0.13$ and the threshold at $10^{-2}/7.9\times10^{-4}=12.7$; Willow reads $A=1$ with its floor at $0.30$ and the
threshold at $3.0$ \calc. The gauge keeps the physical content, the gate error relative to its floor and to the threshold, and nothing else.
