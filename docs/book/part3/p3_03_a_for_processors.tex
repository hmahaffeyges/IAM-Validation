\chapter{The gate error and its floor: quantum processors}\label{ch:ascoreqc}

\section{Two device gauges}\label{sec:devicegauges}

IAM's Law prices every irreversible record at $\kB T\ln2$ per bit at the surface where it is written (Chapter~\ref{ch:iams_law}). Applied to devices,
the law gives two gauges, used in this and the following chapters of Part~\ref{part:3}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The qubit gauge} reads a processor's two-qubit gate error as information lost per gate, $\varepsilon=-\ln(1-p_{2Q})$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:Agate}), against its \emph{thermal floor}: IAM's Law at the temperature the qubit's record actually sees, which for one gate
is $p_{\rm th}=p_{\rm eq}\,t_g/T_1$ with $p_{\rm eq}=1/(1+e^{hf/\kB T})$ (Section~\ref{sec:qubitfloor}). The device as built reads $A=1$; its
floor sits below it at $p_{\rm th}/\varepsilon$, and more error lies to the right.
\item \textbf{The chip gauge} reads a processor's switching energy per transition, $E_{\mathrm{sw}}$, against the Landauer floor
$\kB T_j\ln2$ at the junction temperature $T_j$. The chip as built reads $A=1$; the Landauer floor sits below it at $\kB T_j\ln2/E_{\mathrm{sw}}$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cmos}).
\end{itemize}
Both gauges read one object against two things that belong to it: its own as-built state and the floor physics sets for it. Chapter~\ref{ch:onegauge}
(Part~\ref{part:5}) sets them beside the cell gauges of Part~\ref{part:4}.

\section{From gate error to information cost}

A two-qubit gate with error probability $p$ preserves the intended state with probability
$1-p$. The information lost per gate, in nats, is
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon=-\ln(1-p)=p+\tfrac12p^2+\dots
 \label{eq:Agate}
\end{equation}
For every platform of current interest $p<10^{-2}$, so $\varepsilon$ equals $p$ to better
than 0.5\% \calc. The gate error in nats, $\varepsilon$, is therefore a re-expression of the published error. Its value lies in the
\emph{decomposition} and in the \emph{floor} it is compared with, not in the transformation itself.

\section{The published values}

Table~\ref{tab:platforms} lists published two-qubit errors from the device makers' own reports. They are \observed\ as cited. Two cautions apply to
the whole table. First, published two-qubit errors mix isolated and simultaneous benchmarks,
randomized and cross-entropy benchmarking, and median and mean over the device, and different benchmarks of one processor can give
materially different pictures~\cite{Proctor2022}; differences at the 20\% level are not meaningful without harmonized definitions. Second, the

\noindent For Willow, the error-correction paper~\cite{GoogleWillow2025} shows the CZ error of the 105-qubit processor only as a distribution
(its Fig.~1b), so no single value for that chip enters the table. The paper's error budget for its 72-qubit companion processor lists a CZ
Pauli error of $2.8\times10^{-3}$, excluding CZ crosstalk ($5.5\times10^{-4}$) and leakage ($2.0\times10^{-4}$) (Supplementary Table~S4 of
Ref.~\cite{GoogleWillow2025}) \observed. A Pauli error is a different statistic from the average errors in the table, so it is not entered
there.

\noindent Other platform types have primary published two-qubit values of their own: a fluxonium CZ gate at a mean fidelity of
$99.922\pm0.009$\,\%~\cite{Ding2023}, neutral-atom entangling gates at 99.5\,\% on up to 60 atoms in parallel~\cite{Evered2023}, an NV
electron--nuclear gate at $99.93(5)$\,\%~\cite{Bartling2025} and silicon spin qubits at 99.5\,\%~\cite{Xue2022,Noiri2022} \observed. They are read
on their own gauges in Chapter~\ref{ch:qplatforms}, each with the statistic its paper states.

The ion gates in Table~\ref{tab:platforms} act through the ions' shared motion. The electronic gate applies a
spin-dependent force to the motional modes and works without ground-state cooling~\cite{Hughes2025}. The motional
modes are heated by electric-field noise from the trap electrodes, which grows steeply as the ion approaches the
surface~\cite{Brownnutt2015}; that heating, together with control noise, limits the error of both gate classes.

\section{The floor}\label{sec:qubitfloor}

The left-hand mark of the qubit gauge is the thermal floor of IAM's Law at the temperature the record itself sees. A record held in a gap
$E=hf$ against a bath at temperature $T$ settles at an occupation $p_{\rm eq}=1/(1+e^{hf/\kB T})$ of the wrong level, and one gate of
duration $t_g$ is kicked out of the right level with probability $p_{\rm th}=p_{\rm eq}\,t_g/T_1$ \derived\ (detailed balance;
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:qp_peq} and~\eqref{eq:qp_pth}, Chapter~\ref{ch:qplatforms}). On the gauge the device as built reads $A=1$ and its floor sits
below it at $p_{\rm th}/\varepsilon$. Every input is measured on the device itself: the gap, the temperature the qubit sees, the gate time and
$T_1$; nothing is assigned or fitted.

For a transmon at 5~GHz whose own effective temperature is 35~mK~\cite{Jin2015}, with the mean $T_1=68~\mu$s of the 105-qubit processor of
Ref.~\cite{GoogleWillow2025} and an illustrative 40~ns gate, $p_{\rm th}=6.2\times10^{-7}$ \calc: a two-qubit error near $10^{-3}$ reads its
floor at $6\times10^{-4}$ on its own gauge. For a trapped ion the record is a hyperfine level pair that no bath thermalises, so the floor of the
held record lies far to the left of any gate error; the gate runs through the ions' shared motion, whose thermal channel is the motional heating
described above, and the floor of that channel is set apparatus by apparatus by the heating rate and the gate time
(Chapter~\ref{ch:qplatforms}) \interp. Present gate errors therefore sit orders of magnitude above the floor that temperature alone sets. That
distance is the room the field is working in, and the decomposition of Chapter~\ref{ch:walls} says which loss channel takes it \interp.

Surface-code error correction needs physical error below about $10^{-2}$, and useful
fault-tolerant overhead needs about $10^{-3}$ or better~\cite{Fowler2012,GoogleWillow2025}.
The qubit gauge places each device on one axis with three marks: its thermal floor, the device as built and the error-correction threshold,
with the fault-tolerance target between the last two. Helios reads $A=1$ with the threshold at $10^{-2}/7.9\times10^{-4}=12.7$; the transmon
reading has its floor at $6\times10^{-4}$ and the threshold at 10 \calc. The gauge keeps the physical content, the gate error relative to the
floor temperature sets and to the threshold, and nothing else.
