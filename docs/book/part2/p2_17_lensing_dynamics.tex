\chapter{Lensing mass and dynamical mass}\label{ch:lensdyn}

\section*{The place}
A galaxy cluster: about $10^{14}$--$10^{15}\,M_\odot$ of matter bound on timelike worldlines, crossed by light from galaxies behind it. Matter in the
cluster responds to the Newtonian potential $\Psi$; light responds to the lensing potential $(\Phi+\Psi)/2$. If the informational term changes the first
and not the second, the two mass estimates of one cluster differ, and the difference depends on the redshift of the cluster. Whether the term does
change $\Psi$ inside a cluster depends on the form in which it is implemented (Section~\ref{sec:ld_hold}).

\section*{Summary}
In the $\mu$--$\Sigma$ language, the dual-sector term of Chapters~\ref{ch:latetime} and \ref{ch:level2} gives $\mu(a)<1$ for matter and $\Sigma(a)=1$
for light at linear order. This chapter follows that signature into cluster masses. In the Level~1 form, where $\mu$ multiplies Newton's constant in
the Poisson equation for $\Psi$, a dynamical mass reads $\mu M_{\rm true}$ and a lensing mass reads $M_{\rm true}$, so
$M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}=1/\mu(z)$: 15.8\,\% above unity today and falling monotonically to zero excess at high redshift, with no adjusted
parameter. In the Level~2 form, where the term is a matter-sector expansion rate in the perturbation equations and the linearised Einstein equations
for the potentials are unmodified, $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}=1$ and weak lensing feels the term only through the growth history of $\delta_m$, with
the relation between the Weyl potential and the matter density preserved. The cluster prediction is therefore stated for the Level~1 form, and which
form applies inside clusters is open. The chapter states the prediction, separates it from hydrostatic bias, MOND and other modified-gravity models,
sets it against the published cluster mass ratios, and specifies the redshift-shape test that Euclid, Rubin and eROSITA samples can perform.

\section{The discrepancy}\label{sec:ld_intro}
Cluster masses estimated from gravitational lensing are larger than those estimated from dynamical methods, galaxy velocity dispersions and X-ray
hydrostatic equilibrium~\cite{Mahdavi2013,Israel2014,Hoekstra2015,vonderLinden2014,Smith2016LoCuSS}. The usual reading is hydrostatic bias: the
assumption that the intracluster medium is in exact hydrostatic equilibrium neglects non-thermal pressure from bulk motions, turbulence and cosmic
rays. The standard parametrisation is
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm true}=\frac{M_{X\text{-ray}}}{1-b},
\label{eq:ld_b}\end{equation}
with estimates of $b$ from about $0.1$ to about $0.4$ depending on the method~\cite{Nagai2007ICM,Rasia2012,Biffi2016}. \observed

The hydrostatic reading has three features that leave room for another contribution. The bias required by the Planck cluster counts is larger than
hydrodynamic simulations give: simulations find $b\approx0.1$--$0.15$~\cite{Lau2009,Nelson2014}, while reconciling the Planck SZ counts with the
primary CMB in $\Lambda$CDM needs $1-b=0.58\pm0.04$ (Planck 2015 XXIV~\cite{Planck2015SZ}). \observed\ The weak-lensing calibrations do not agree with
each other at the level of that difference (Table~\ref{tab:ld_published}). And the comparison of lensing masses with Planck masses changes with
redshift in the like-for-like reanalysis of Smith et al.~\cite{Smith2016LoCuSS} (Section~\ref{sec:ld_data}). Together these suggest that the
explanation may not be purely astrophysical. \interp

The proposal of this chapter is that the dual-sector term produces a lensing--dynamics mass discrepancy of the right order and with a redshift
dependence that the astrophysical explanations do not share. It follows from the dual-sector signature: $\mu<1$ (a weaker gravitational coupling for
matter) with $\Sigma=1$ (unmodified light deflection). The same signature, written into the Boltzmann code as a matter-sector expansion rate, gives
$\Delta\chi^2=+0.54$ against Planck relative to $\Lambda$CDM with $\sigma_8=0.7998\pm0.0058$ (the Level~2 chains of Chapter~\ref{ch:level2}; 18
converged chains in all, Chapter~\ref{ch:dual}). \measured

\section{The prediction}\label{sec:ld_prediction}
\subsection{The $\mu$--$\Sigma$ framework}
In the standard parametrisation of modified gravity~\cite{PogosianSilvestri2016,Amendola2008}, deviations from general relativity at linear order
are carried by two functions of the scale factor:
\begin{align}
\nabla^2\Psi&=4\pi Ga^2\,\mu(a)\,\bar\rho\,\delta\qquad\text{(Poisson equation; dynamics)},\label{eq:ld_poisson}\\
\nabla^2(\Phi+\Psi)&=8\pi Ga^2\,\Sigma(a)\,\bar\rho\,\delta\qquad\text{(lensing potential)},\label{eq:ld_lens}
\end{align}
where $\Psi$ is the Newtonian potential that governs the motion of matter and $\Phi+\Psi$ is the lensing (Weyl) potential that governs the
deflection of light. In general relativity, $\mu=\Sigma=1$. \derived\ (definitions)

\subsection{The IAM values}
The dual-sector term maps onto
\begin{equation}
\mu(a)=\frac{H^2_\Lambda(a)}{H^2_\Lambda(a)+\beta_mE(a)H_0^2},\qquad\Sigma(a)=1\quad\text{(all redshifts)},
\label{eq:ld_mu}\end{equation}
with $H^2_\Lambda(a)=H_0^2(\Omega_ma^{-3}+\Omega_\Lambda)$, the coupling $\beta_m=\Omega_m/2=0.15765$ set by the virial partition
(Chapter~\ref{ch:virial}) \prediction, and the activation function $E(a)=\exp(1-1/a)$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}. The mapping from the matter-sector
rate to $\mu$ is given in Appendix~\ref{app:der:growth}. \interp\ No parameter is fitted.

The physical reading of $\Sigma=1$: photons travel on null geodesics, along which no proper time elapses ($d\tau=0$), so they do not take part in
gravitational decoherence. The informational contribution that modifies the matter sector is invisible to light, and lensing sees the full,
unmodified gravitational potential. \interp

\subsection{The mass ratio}
\textbf{Step 1 (dynamical mass).} A dynamical mass is read from the acceleration that matter feels. For a spherical cluster, Gauss's law applied to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_poisson} gives $|\nabla\Psi|(r)=G\mu M_{\rm true}(<r)/r^2$, and an observer who assumes general relativity infers
$M_{\rm dyn}(<r)=r^2|\nabla\Psi|/G$:
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm dyn}\propto\Psi\propto\mu\,M_{\rm true}.
\label{eq:ld_mdyn}\end{equation}
\textbf{Step 2 (lensing mass).} A lensing mass is read from the deflection, which is set by the gradient of $(\Psi+\Phi)/2$. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_lens},
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm lens}\propto\frac{\Psi+\Phi}{2}\propto\Sigma\,M_{\rm true}=M_{\rm true}.
\label{eq:ld_mlens}\end{equation}
\textbf{Step 3 (ratio).} Dividing,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\frac{M_{\rm lens}}{M_{\rm dyn}}=\frac{\Sigma}{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu(z)}}\,.
\label{eq:ld_ratio_mu}\end{equation}
\derived\ (in the Level~1 form, where $\mu$ stands in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_poisson}). The whole curve $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}(z)$ is fixed by
$\beta_m=\Omega_m/2$ and $E(a)=\exp(1-1/a)$; it is a zero-parameter prediction. \prediction

\subsection{Explicit functional form}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_mu} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio_mu}, the $H_0^2$ cancels:
\begin{equation}
\frac{M_{\rm lens}}{M_{\rm dyn}}(a)=1+\frac{\beta_mE(a)H_0^2}{H^2_\Lambda(a)}=1+\frac{\beta_m\exp(1-1/a)}{\Omega_ma^{-3}+\Omega_\Lambda}.
\label{eq:ld_ratio}\end{equation}
\derived\ (sympy: $1/\mu-(1+\beta_mE/H^2)=0$). This is the observable prediction: a monotonically declining function of redshift, fixed by the
Planck 2018 parameters and the virial partition, with no fitted parameter. Its slope is $dR/dz=-0.31$ at $z=0$, $-0.18$ at $z=0.3$, $-0.12$ at
$z=0.5$ and $-0.04$ at $z=1$ ($R\equiv M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$). It depends on the cluster's redshift only, not on its mass, radius or dynamical
state. \calc

\subsection{The two potentials in the Level 1 form}\label{sec:ld_slip}
Equations~\eqref{eq:ld_poisson} and \eqref{eq:ld_lens} with $\Sigma=1$ fix both potentials. Subtracting the first from the second,
$\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi Ga^2(2-\mu)\bar\rho\delta$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}=\frac{2-\mu}{\mu},
\label{eq:ld_slip}\end{equation}
\derived\ (sympy). With $\mu<1$ the two potentials differ: $\Phi/\Psi=1.315$ today, $1.170$ at $z=0.3$, $1.036$ at $z=1$. \calc\ In the Level~1 form
the term therefore acts as an effective gravitational slip, the signature of an anisotropic stress in the metric equations, while the Weyl potential
$\Phi+\Psi$ keeps its general-relativistic relation to $\delta$. A form with unmodified linearised Einstein equations has $\Phi=\Psi$ and $\mu=1$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_poisson}. The two statements, a cluster mass ratio $1/\mu$ and potentials that obey the unmodified Einstein equations, cannot both hold;
Section~\ref{sec:ld_hold} takes this up.

\section{The form of the term}\label{sec:ld_hold}
The ratio~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio} needs $\mu$ in the Poisson equation for $\Psi$ (the Level~1 effective-coupling form, the form MGCAMB implements,
Chapter~\ref{ch:latetime}). In the Level~2 form the term is a matter-sector expansion rate in the perturbation equations (Chapter~\ref{ch:level2}):
the linearised Einstein equations for the metric potentials are unmodified, the informational term introduces no anisotropic stress and no
perturbative degree of freedom, and $\Phi=\Psi$. Then $\Psi$ obeys the standard Poisson equation, matter in a virialised cluster moves in the true
potential, and $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}=1$ at every redshift. In that form weak-lensing observables feel the term only indirectly, through the altered
growth history of $\delta_m$, and the relation between the Weyl potential and the matter density is preserved; the CMB lensing power, for example, is
lower by $0.08\,\%$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:surveys}). \derived\ (structure of the equations)

The cluster prediction is therefore stated in the Level~1 form only. Two questions are open: which form is the physical one, and whether a linear
$\mu$ applies inside virialised, non-linear clusters at all. \openprob

\section{Other explanations and how they differ}\label{sec:ld_alternatives}
\paragraph{Hydrostatic bias.} A constant bias gives a constant ratio $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}=1/(1-b)$, $1.205$ for $b=0.17$ \calc, independent of
redshift by construction. Cluster evolution can add a redshift dependence through the relaxation state, but that would correlate with indicators of
dynamical state and would not follow the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio}. Simulations find the non-thermal pressure fraction rising with redshift,
because younger clusters are less relaxed~\cite{Nelson2014,ShiKomatsu2014}; the bias then grows with $z$, the opposite slope. \observed\
\emph{Distinguishing test:} plot $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$ against $z$. Hydrostatic bias gives a flat line, or a rising one, with scatter;
the Level~1 form gives the declining curve~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio}. \prediction

\paragraph{MOND and its relativistic extensions.} These predict enhanced gravitational effects at low acceleration, $a<a_0\approx1.2\times10^{-10}$\,m\,s$^{-2}$.
The mass discrepancy then depends on the acceleration profile of each cluster, not on its cosmological redshift. \emph{Distinguishing test:} at fixed
cluster mass and morphology, MOND makes $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$ depend on position within the cluster; the Level~1 form makes it depend on the
cluster's redshift only, independent of its internal properties. \prediction

\paragraph{Modified gravity in the $\mu$--$\Sigma$ form.} In the quasi-static limit $f(R)$ gravity has $\Sigma=1$ and $1\le\mu\le4/3$, scale-dependent~\cite{PogosianSilvestri2016}: the lensing mass equals the true mass and the dynamical mass is larger, $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}\le1$, the opposite of
what is observed. The normal branch of DGP has $\mu>1$ and $\Sigma=1$, scale-independent in the quasi-static limit. The self-accelerating branch of
DGP has $\mu<1$ and $\Sigma=1$, the same signs as IAM, but carries a ghost~\cite{Koyama2007} and is in tension with CMB, supernova and $H_0$
data~\cite{Fang2008}. \observed\ The signs $\mu<1$, $\Sigma=1$ are therefore not unique to IAM. Its distinct content is the parameter-free curve
\eqref{eq:ld_ratio}, which in IAM comes from a physical asymmetry between timelike and null worldlines rather than from parameter
choice. \interp

\paragraph{Combined test design.} Compare $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$ in at least five redshift bins over $0.1<z<2$ with one mass method throughout:
\begin{enumerate}
\item select clusters with both weak-lensing and spectroscopic velocity-dispersion measurements;
\item fit the same NFW profile model to both;
\item compute $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$ in each bin with bootstrap errors;
\item fit three shapes: a constant (hydrostatic bias), Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio} with no free parameter, and a power law $1+A(1+z)^n$ (two free parameters);
\item compare them by BIC and AIC.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Connection to the dual-sector cosmology}\label{sec:ld_cosmo}
The lensing--dynamics ratio is one face of the split between matter and photon observables. The same $\mu<1$, $\Sigma=1$ signature, with
$\beta_m=\Omega_m/2$ and $E(a)=\exp(1-1/a)$ and nothing added, gives a matter-sector Hubble constant $72.26$ against the photon-sector $67.16$
km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:level2}), the lower $\sigma_8$ ($0.8087\to0.7998$ at Level~2), and $f\sigma_8$ lower by $4.25\,\%$,
$2.17\,\%$, $1.35\,\%$ and $0.41\,\%$ at $z=0$, $0.3$, $0.5$ and $1$ (form (i) of Appendix~\ref{app:der:growth}). \calc\ The cluster test has three
strengths: it is measurable with current and near-future data, it does not depend on CMB modelling, and it has a distinctive shape in redshift. It
carries one condition the other results do not: the Hubble-rate split and $\sigma_8$ come from the Level~2 mechanism, in which the cluster ratio is 1,

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:ld_discussion}
\paragraph{Systematics.} The main systematic in any lensing--dynamics comparison is the calibration of the two masses. Weak-lensing masses depend on
the source redshift distribution, shape-measurement systematics and projection. Dynamical masses depend on assumptions about velocity anisotropy and
dynamical equilibrium. X-ray masses depend on the temperature calibration and the hydrostatic assumption. The prediction concerns the ratio
$M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$, which cancels many systematics that affect absolute masses. The redshift dependence adds discriminating power: systematics
that evolve with $z$ would have to mimic the specific form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio}, for which no known systematic gives a physical reason.
\interp

\paragraph{Prior work.} The ratio of dynamical to lensing response has been constrained from cluster mass comparisons for chameleon and $f(R)$
gravity~\cite{Terukina2014,Wilcox2015,Pizzuti2017}. These analyses fit constant parameters rather than redshift-dependent functions. The Level~1
prediction, $\mu(a)$ falling from 1 at high redshift to $0.864$ at $z=0$ with $\Sigma=1$, has not been tested in this way. \observed

\paragraph{Falsifiability.} In the Level~1 form the prediction is explicitly falsifiable. \prediction
\begin{itemize}
\item If $M_{\rm lens}/M_{\rm dyn}$ shows no redshift dependence in a clean, uniformly measured sample, hydrostatic bias is preferred.
\item If it rises with $z$ after the non-thermal part is modelled, the gravitational part is absent or of the other sign.
\item If its value at $z=0$ differs from $1.158$ by more than $3\sigma$ in a sample large enough, the normalisation is challenged.
\item If the redshift dependence follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio}, the comparison is a direct measurement of $\mu(z)$ from clusters.
\end{itemize}
Whatever the surveys find, the comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ld_ratio} is unambiguous once the implementation form is fixed.

\paragraph{The dual-sector construction.} $\Sigma=1$ follows from the dual-sector construction: the informational contribution renormalises only the
effective expansion rate of the matter sector, so the photon sector and the relation between the Weyl potential and the density are those of
$\Lambda$CDM. That keeps growth and lensing observables consistent with one another in both forms. \derived\ (structure of the equations)

The redshift-binned lensing-to-dynamical mass ratio is a test anyone with cluster lensing and velocity-dispersion data can run now. A cosmologist with the coming cluster catalogues does not have to wait to find out where it lands.
