\chapter{Serial mode: one person, many draws}\label{ch:serial}

\section{The strongest measurement is a difference}

The author's view of where the instrument's value lies is plain: \emph{it has always been about the sequence of tests for individual patients;
they will have a trajectory from draw to draw that shows progression or not, and they will create the best sky map as well.} There is a
measurement reason to agree. Everything that is constant in a person between two draws, their genotype, their laboratory's pipeline if it is
held fixed, the atlas's errors at their composition, cancels in a difference. In CMB language, two draws of one person are two maps of one sky,
and their difference is the split-map null test turned into a signal.

\section{What serial mode computes}

Given a draw and an earlier draw of the same person (same identifier hash, same array type, same pipeline; otherwise the pair is refused):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Per-cell $\Delta A$} for every cell present in both draws, with the cell's fraction in each. A cell present in one draw only is listed as
      appeared or disappeared, not differenced.
\item \textbf{The difference sky}, $\Delta\beta_i=\beta_i^{\mathrm{now}}-\beta_i^{\mathrm{prior}}$ at every address, drawn on the same projection as the sky.
      No atlas expectation, no $\sigma$ from any population and no composition enter: two measurements of one person and their difference.
\item \textbf{A trajectory} when there are three or more draws: each cell's $A$ by date, the sign of the last step, and whether any step exceeded the change floor.
      No fit is made to a trajectory and no population defines its expected shape.
\end{enumerate}
Composition drops out of a difference only in part: if a person's fractions change between draws, the difference sky carries that change. The out-of-span
projection of Chapter~\ref{ch:sky} removes it exactly, which is why its fourth test is written on serial pairs.

\section{The change floor}

A difference means nothing without the size of a difference that noise alone produces. That is set by technical replicates: the same person's DNA
measured twice. The only large public set of whole-blood 450K replicates with the raw intensities deposited is the LOLIPOP study's 36 replicate pairs
(GSE55763)~\cite{Lehne2015}. It was streamed column by column, keeping only the 72 replicate arrays.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/part4/fig_serial_floor.pdf}
\caption{Candidate change floor for serial readings: the 0.99 quantile of $|\Delta A|$ between technical replicates, per blood cell present in both arrays
of a pair ($n$ = pairs). Cells present in fewer than five pairs are omitted. \measured}
\label{fig:serialfloor}
\end{figure}

The per-cell floors are 0.066--0.080 in $A$ (Figure~\ref{fig:serialfloor}); the per-address floor on $\Delta\beta$ is 0.091. \measured{} These are upper bounds, for
two reasons recorded with them. The deposited intensities skip the chain's background correction. And every one of the 36 pairs was run on different chips: in 33
pairs every cell's $\Delta A$ had the same sign, a shift of the whole array between chip runs, not per-cell noise. The ten or so pairs with no such offset differ by
at most 0.015 in any cell's $A$. The floor that matters for a laboratory running serial draws on one platform is therefore likely closer to 0.015 than to 0.07. A set
of same-chip replicates through the chain's own Stage~1 would measure it. \openprob

\section{A first difference sky}

The Swedish Adoption/Twin Study of Aging (SATSA) deposited raw arrays for individuals drawn up to five times over two decades
(E-MTAB-7309)~\cite{Wang2018SATSA}. Figure~\ref{fig:satsa} shows one individual's three later draws against the first, with only the addresses beyond the replicate
floor coloured.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/part4/SATSA_IND_155_difference_sky_FLOORED.png}
\caption{Difference sky for one SATSA individual, three later draws against the first (ages 72.6 to 79.6, 82.6 and 88.4). Only addresses whose $|\Delta\beta|$
exceeds the replicate floor of 0.091 carry colour (red up, blue down). By construction about 1~\% of addresses would exceed it between replicates; 8--10~\% do here.
Draws 1 and 2 of this individual fall below the intake call-rate line. \measured}
\label{fig:satsa}
\end{figure}

About 8--10~\% of addresses change by more than the replicate floor across 7 to 16 years, against about 1~\% expected between replicates, with more addresses losing
methylation than gaining it. \measured{} Two cautions go with the picture. Most SATSA draws fall below the intake line of Chapter~\ref{ch:instrument}, so every point
of a SATSA trajectory must carry its intake status; this individual's first two draws are among them. And the floor is an upper bound from arrays on different chips.
The picture shows what a serial reading looks like; it is not yet a measurement of how fast a person's methylome moves.

\section{Why serial mode also makes the atlas better}

A person measured repeatedly is their own reference at every address. With enough draws, the per-address spread of one person between draws is a noise map made
with no population at all, and differences from it are that person's changes. The same draws, pooled over many people, would say which addresses are stable in
every person and which move: information the atlas does not yet carry. Neither use requires comparing one person with another.
