\chapter{Falsifiable predictions}\label{ch:predictions}

\section*{How this list was made}
We collected every quantitative statement the framework makes, 557 in all, in one register (\path{CANON/predictions_register.csv} in the
repository, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). The 136 cell entries wait for the commissioning of each cell type and are not listed here. Each of the other
421 was recomputed and given one verdict: kept, with its value recomputed where needed (67), already compared with data (54), a duplicate of another entry
(143), or dropped (157). An entry was dropped when it is not falsifiable (a definition, a restatement, an interpretation), when the recomputation
does not support it, when it is a vendor roadmap or commercial statement with no physics in it, or when no test is named. The verdict for every
entry, with its reason, is in \path{CANON/predictions_triage_2026-10-02.json}.
Appendix~\ref{app:register} lists every entry that stands, with its test, its falsifier and its verdict.

What remains is about thirty distinct predictions. Each is given below with its number, the measurement that tests it, what would falsify it,
and the register entries it merges (in brackets). Every value is recomputed. Dates are those of the named surveys; none is claimed for an
experiment no one has announced. A prediction built on a calibrated input says so: it tests the input as much as the law.

\paragraph{Status labels.} \Spassed{} IAM's own equations, with no free parameter, were run against the data and came out as predicted.
\Sprecision{} The data agree, and their errors are still wider than the effect; the next survey decides. \Spending{} The test is set and its data
are not in yet. \Stension{} The data currently disagree.

\section{Device floors}
The device gauges of Part~\ref{part:3} read every device against a floor that temperature alone sets: for a qubit gate the thermal floor
$p_{\rm eq}t_g/T_1$ with $p_{\rm eq}=1/(1+e^{hf/\kB T})$ at the temperature the record sees (Chapter~\ref{ch:qplatforms}), for a chip the Landauer
floor $\kB T_j\ln2$ at its junction (Chapter~\ref{ch:cmos}). These floors are derived, not predicted: a gate kicked out of its level by thermal
noise less often than $p_{\rm eq}t_g/T_1$, or an irreversible switch that costs less than $\kB T_j\ln2$, would contradict detailed balance or
Landauer's principle at that surface. \derived{} The device tests that are open are measurements on one device, and any laboratory or fab can
make them: the coherence optimum on one material and one coupler topology (Chapter~\ref{ch:walls}), the drive optima of ion and Rydberg gates
(Chapters~\ref{ch:walls} and~\ref{ch:qplatforms}), and how far each loss channel sits above the floor of the bath that drives it. Each needs the
gap, the temperature the record sees, the gate time, $T_1$ and the isolated two-qubit error of one device, published together; for a chip, one
stated power, count and clock on both sides of a comparison (Chapter~\ref{ch:chipgen}). \openprob

\section{What the list shows}
The cosmology carries clear tests, and the surveys that make them are under way. One parameter-free line fixes $\mu_0$, the $f\sigma_8$ ramp, $\Sigma=1$, $E_G$,
$S_8$ and the two Hubble rates together; growth surveys through the mid-2030s measure its size to about $2.5$--$3\sigma$ (Section~\ref{sec:sp_euclid}). The device floors are derived, and the open device tests are
measurements that one laboratory can make on one device. The decoherence predictions are the most distinctive, a coherence time that
grows with temperature, and the furthest from current experiments.

The predictions are dated, recomputed and tied to named surveys and experiments. Pick one in your field and test it.
